\subsection{A semi-abelian group which is not monomial: Problem 21.68}
\label{subsec:semiabelian}
\problemstatement{21.68}

A finite group is \emph{monomial} if each irreducible complex
representation is induced from a one-dimensional representation of
a subgroup. In the definition used in \kn{21.68}, semi-abelian
groups are obtained along a subgroup chain by abelian semidirect
extensions and quotients. The construction below uses only split
extensions with abelian kernels.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:semiabelian}
There is a semi-abelian group of order $2,592$ with a non-monomial
irreducible complex representation of degree eight.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $E$ be the even-weight subspace of $\F_2^4$ and let $A_4$
permute its coordinates. Put $K=E\rtimes A_4$, with multiplication
$(v,g)(w,h)=(v+g(w),gh)$. In $K$, set
\[
z=(1111,1),\quad
x=(1010,(12)(34)),\quad
y=(1001,(13)(24)),\quad
r=(0000,(234)).
\]
Direct multiplication gives
\[
x^2=y^2=(xy)^2=z,\qquad
z^2=1,\qquad r^3=1,\qquad
rxr^{-1}=y,\qquad ryr^{-1}=xy.
\]
The eight elements $1,z,x,zx,y,zy,xy,zxy$ are distinct:
their permutation parts form the Klein four group, with two
distinct vectors above each part. Thus $\langle x,y\rangle$
is quaternion of order eight and
$H=\langle x,y,r\rangle$ has order 24.
Its abelianization is $C_3$: in an abelian quotient the
conjugation relations give $x=y=xy$, whence $x=y=1$.
In particular, $H$ has no subgroup of index two.

The group $H$ has an irreducible complex representation $\rho$
of degree two. To see this explicitly, represent $x,y$ by the
quaternion units $i,j$ and $r$ by
$(-1-i-j-k)/2$. The latter has order three and conjugates
$i$ to $j$ and $j$ to $k=ij$.
The usual two-dimensional complex representation of the
quaternions is irreducible: $i$ has the two coordinate
eigenlines and $j$ interchanges them.

Let $\Omega=K/H$, a set of four left cosets, and put
\[
B=\left\{b\in\F_3^\Omega:\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}b_\omega=0\right\},
\qquad G=B\rtimes K.
\]
Then $|G|=27\cdot96=2,592$. The chain
\[
1<C_3<A_4<K<G
\]
exhibits the required abelian split extensions.
Fix $\omega_0=H$ and a primitive cube root of unity $\zeta$.
The character $\lambda(b)=\zeta^{b_{\omega_0}}$ has stabilizer
exactly $H$ in $K$. Indeed, the four coordinate characters on
$B$ are distinct: for distinct coordinates $u,v$, choose a
third coordinate $w$ and take $b_u=1$, $b_w=-1$, with all
other coordinates zero. Hence the inertia subgroup of
$\lambda$ in $G$ is $I=B\rtimes H$.

The representation
$\tau(b,h)=\lambda(b)\rho(h)$ of $I$ is well-defined because
$H$ fixes $\lambda$. Let $V=\Ind_I^G\tau$. Its restriction
to $B$ has four distinct weights, each of multiplicity two.
The group $G$ permutes their weight spaces transitively,
and the stabilizer acts irreducibly on each such space.
Every invariant subspace splits into $B$-weight spaces,
using the averaging projections for the finite abelian
group $B$. Therefore $V$ is irreducible and has dimension
eight.

Suppose $V\cong\Ind_L^G\mu$ with $\mu$ one-dimensional.
Then $[G:L]=8$. Since $B$ is a normal 3-group,
$[B:B\cap L]=[BL:L]$ is a power of three dividing eight;
thus $B\le L$. The character $\mu|_B$ must be one of the
four weights of $V|_B$. After conjugation, assume it is
$\lambda$. A linear character is invariant under
conjugation by its own domain, so $L$ stabilizes $\lambda$.
Thus $L\le I$ and
\[
[I:L]=[G:L]/[G:I]=8/4=2.
\]
Because $B\le L$, this gives an index-two subgroup of
$I/B\cong H$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Kida's paper~\cite{kida} provided the small order-96 configuration
and posed the monomiality conjecture. The abelian extension and
the character obstruction are the proposed additional argument
in the experimental archive. The proof does not rely on a
SmallGroups identifier. That identifier and separate finite
checks are useful for reproduction, not for the logical
existence of the example.
