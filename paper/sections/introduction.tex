\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The Kourovka Notebook~\cite{kourovka21} collects open problems in group
theory and neighboring areas, ranging from concrete questions about finite
groups to structural and logical problems about infinite ones. We present
candidate answers for 45 problem entries or specified subparts, partial
results on nine questions, and rediscoveries and deductions from earlier
work, obtained in a 48-hour experiment with an AI research agent.
The archive includes finite certificates and computational checks.
A second language model subsequently reviewed the mathematical work
independently. The deadline ledger contained 46 candidates; version~3
withdrew the claimed answer to 10.35, which misread
\(\overline{\Q}\) as \(\Q\). Evgeny Khukhro, the Notebook's editor,
identified this error after both model reviews had accepted the entry.
The eleven-entry comparison with contemporary solutions added in
version~2 is retained. Version~4 adds a mathematical edition,
\emph{Results on problems from the Kourovka Notebook}, compiled from
the same statements, proofs and attribution notes (Appendix~\ref{app:two-editions}).

Michael Kinyon and Josef Urban conceived and oversaw the experiment.
They gave OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, running through Codex, an explicit
48-hour deadline and requested local resource limits of generally
20 CPU cores and 100\,GB of memory. With access to GAP, further software
and the literature, the agent could choose which questions listed as open
in that edition to pursue. The aim was to evaluate current AI and automated
reasoning capabilities on open research problems under a shared budget:
selecting questions, interpreting hypotheses, developing arguments and
checking the resulting claims.

The experiment has two phases. During 10--12 September 2026, Codex
selected problems, developed arguments and checked its own work. After
manuscript preparation, Anthropic's Claude Opus[1m], running through
Claude Code, independently reviewed the paper and then responded to the
authors' reply and supporting material. Claude examined all 46 entries
on the deadline candidate list, reported verifying a majority in detail, and
independently rebuilt many of the finite constructions and calculations.
These checks provide evidence for the arguments examined, but the later
10.35 correction shows that agreement between the models did not ensure
that each argument answered the original question.
The corrections, confirmations and exchanges with
Codex form a second part of the experiment, described in
Section~\ref{sec:external-review}.

The number 46 records the problem entries classified as complete solution
candidates at the deadline. One of these, 10.35, is withdrawn in this
version, leaving 45 other candidates. Neither total is a count of
established new solutions. The ledger counts specified subparts once per entry,
and some entries share a construction or a substantial imported theorem.
The inventory in Section~\ref{app:inventory} gives the covered parts and
convention-sensitive hypotheses. Subsequent review provides evidence of
correctness; novelty is a separate question. The comparison in
Appendix~\ref{app:contemporary-comparison} identifies overlapping work
on eleven entries, including the previously acknowledged 21.106.
Some constructions agree closely; others differ or yield stronger results.
We assign no discovery priority from the available chronology, and do not
infer that the remaining entries are new. Known results and deductions
retain their prior-work status; the qualitative computability assertion
in 16.9 is now explicitly identified as a consequence of older work.
The nine partial-result questions treated
in Section~\ref{app:partials} are 20.100, 20.89/19.83, 21.121(b), 20.124,
17.118, 21.114, 19.108, 18.120 and 6.47. Here 20.89/19.83 is one repeated
question, and 21.121(a) is already among the 46 entries. That section also
records bounded searches on five other questions.

\subsection{Motivation and reading routes}

The design follows the use of explicit time and resource budgets in ATP
competitions and human mathematical contests such as the International
Mathematical Olympiad~\cite{imo-regulations}. Urban's specific inspiration
was the MPTPChallenge, designed in 2006, twenty years before this
experiment~\cite{mptpchallenge}: attempt related problems under a shared
budget and reuse discoveries across them. Here the challenge concerns
open research problems, with freedom to select questions, consult sources
and choose computational tools. We omit a comprehensive survey of the
rapidly growing work on learning-guided proving and LLM-based AI for
mathematics.

The same experimenters subsequently ran Anthropic's Claude Fable~5.1
on the same machine, task, 48-hour budget and requested local resource
limits~\cite{fable-kourovka}. Appendix~\ref{app:parallel-fable} gives
a preliminary mathematical review and comparison of the two efforts.

After the inventory, AI and automated reasoning readers can continue to
Sections~\ref{sec:external-review}--\ref{sec:trajectory} and
\ref{sec:discussion}. Mathematical readers can select proofs from the
inventory or begin with Section~\ref{sec:examples}.
