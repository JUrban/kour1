# Problem 21.99: elementary positive cases and reductions

2026-09-12. These are partial results; their priority is unclaimed.
The arbitrary-degree question remains unresolved here.

Write P(G,Omega) for the requested property: for every distinct pair
alpha,beta there is g taking alpha to beta with |Fix_Omega(g)| != 1.
All actions below are finite and transitive. Replacing a group by its
permutation image does not change the question.

## Prime-power degree

**Proposition.** Every transitive permutation group of degree p^a, a>=1,
has property P.

Fix alpha and put H=G_alpha. Choose Q in Syl_p(H), and choose P in
Syl_p(G) containing Q. Then P intersect H=Q, since Q already has the
largest possible p-power order in H. Therefore

    |alpha^P| = |P:Q| = |G|_p/|H|_p = |G:H| = p^a.

Thus P is transitive. For any beta choose g in P taking alpha to beta.
Every nontrivial cycle of g has length divisible by p, so its number of
fixed points is congruent to |Omega|=p^a modulo p. It cannot be one.
This proves the proposition. It requires neither solvability of G nor a
regular Sylow subgroup.

## Lifting through a semiregular normal subgroup

A subgroup N is semiregular if only its identity fixes a point.

**Lemma.** Let N be a nontrivial semiregular normal subgroup of G, and
let B be its orbit set. If the induced group on B has P (with the
one-point case understood vacuously), then G has P on Omega.

First note a fact about any g in G. Its centralizer C_N(g) preserves
Fix_Omega(g) and acts semiregularly there. Hence

    |C_N(g)| divides |Fix_Omega(g)|.

If |C_N(g)|>1, this already excludes exactly one fixed point. Suppose
instead that C_N(g)=1. Every N-orbit fixed setwise by g contains exactly
one fixed point of g. To prove this without a coprimality hypothesis,
write that orbit as delta N and delta^g=delta a, where a in N. Put
phi(n)=g^(-1) n g. Then delta n is fixed by g precisely when

    a phi(n) = n,  or equivalently  phi(n)n^(-1)=a^(-1).

The map n -> phi(n)n^(-1) is injective: equality for n and m gives
phi(m^(-1)n)=m^(-1)n, hence m=n because C_N(g)=1. Finiteness makes
this map bijective. The assertion follows. Consequently in this case
g has exactly as many fixed points as its induced permutation of B.

Now let alpha and beta be distinct. If they are in the same N-orbit,
the unique n in N taking alpha to beta is nonidentity and has no fixed
point. Otherwise choose a good induced permutation taking alpha N to
beta N. Any lift g maps these two blocks. Multiplying g on the right
by a suitable n in N arranges alpha^(gn)=beta and leaves the induced
permutation unchanged. Its fixed-block count is different from one.
The preceding dichotomy, applied to gn, shows its fixed-point count is
also different from one. This proves the lemma.

Normality, semiregularity and finiteness are used explicitly. An
arbitrary abelian normal subgroup need not be semiregular, so this does
not prove the general solvable case.

**Corollaries.** Every transitive nilpotent permutation group has P.
More generally P holds whenever successive induced actions can be
reduced to degree one by taking orbits of nontrivial semiregular normal
subgroups. A smallest-degree counterexample can have no nontrivial
semiregular normal subgroup, and in particular must have trivial centre.

For the nilpotent assertion, work with the faithful image. A nontrivial
central element fixing one point fixes every point by transitivity,
and thus is the identity. The nontrivial centre is therefore
semiregular. The induced action on its orbits is again nilpotent and
has smaller degree; induction and the lemma apply. The same argument
proves the statements about smallest degree and the centre.

## Passing to larger groups and Cartesian products

If a transitive subgroup L of G has P on the same set, then its
witnesses also prove P for G. In particular, a regular subgroup gives
the stronger conclusion with zero fixed points.

If each G_i has P on Omega_i, then their direct product in Cartesian
product action has P. Given distinct tuples, choose one differing
coordinate and use its P witness there; use any transporting elements
in the other coordinates. The number of fixed tuples is the product
of the coordinate fixed-point counts. A factor which is zero or at
least two prevents the product from being one. The same conclusion
holds for any permutation overgroup of this direct product.

## Why generator-word certificates prove all ordered pairs

Suppose explicit words w_beta in the supplied generators take a fixed
point alpha_0 to every beta != alpha_0, each with fixed-point count
different from one. These words first prove transitivity of the
generated group. Given distinct alpha,beta, choose t taking alpha to
alpha_0. Let w take alpha_0 to beta^t. Then t*w*t^(-1) takes alpha to
beta, and conjugacy preserves the number of fixed points. Thus the
finite list of words proves P for the entire supplied action.

The verifier evaluates every multiplication and inverse in these words
as permutations; it does not assume that the producer's orbit or
stabilizer calculations were correct. The catalogue's completeness and
labelling remain imported when this is converted into a degree bound.
