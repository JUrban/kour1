# 14.72: a smooth fixed divisor with a singular cyclic quotient

Status: complete candidate negative answer to the printed question. The
group has order four and the surface is smooth, irreducible and affine over
the complex numbers. Outside review and novelty checks remain pending.

The supplied Notebook page 80 was visually checked. It requires a finite
**cyclic** group, with no prime-order restriction, and calls the fixed locus
a regular hypersurface, explicitly specifying codimension one. The fixed
locus below is a smooth effective Cartier divisor isomorphic to an affine
line. No assumption that the action is free outside that divisor is printed.

## The surface and action

Work over k=C, with i^2=-1. Let X be the closed affine surface in A^4 with
coordinates x,y,u,v and equations

    y^2 = x^3-x,
    yu = xv,
    (x^2-1)u = yv.                                        (1)

Define

    sigma(x,y,u,v) = (-x, iy, iu, v),       G=<sigma>.

The three defining polynomials in (1) are multiplied, respectively, by
-1, -1 and i. Thus sigma is an automorphism of X. Its fourth power is the
identity and its square is not the identity, so G is cyclic of order four.

Write C for the affine curve y^2=x^3-x. This curve is smooth: a singular
point would have y=0 and 3x^2=1, whereas points with y=0 have x=0,1,-1.
The polynomial x^3-x is not a square in k(x), so C is irreducible.

Projection X -> C is a line bundle, as can be checked without bundle theory.
The following two principal open sets cover C and X:

    U1: x != 0,           U2: x^2-1 != 0.

On U1, the second equation eliminates v=yu/x and the third then follows
from the curve equation. Thus U1 in X is C_x times A^1_u. On U2, the third
equation eliminates u=yv/(x^2-1), and the second follows from the curve
equation. Thus U2 in X is C_(x^2-1) times A^1_v. Their overlap is nonempty.
These coordinate-ring identifications also rule out any hidden components
or nonreduced structure in (1). They prove that X is a smooth irreducible
affine surface.

## The whole fixed scheme is a smooth Cartier divisor

Since -1 and i are different from 1, the fixed-point equations are exactly

    x=y=u=0.

Equation (1) then imposes no condition on v. In particular, even the
scheme-theoretic fixed locus is

    X^G = {(0,0,0,v): v in k} ~= A^1.

It is contained in U2. There x=y^2/(x^2-1) and u=yv/(x^2-1), so its ideal
(x,y,u) is generated by y. Since U2 is integral and y is nonzero, this is
a non-zero-divisor. Hence X^G is a smooth effective Cartier divisor in X,
of codimension one, with no additional isolated fixed points or components.
Here hypersurface is used in the printed codimension-one sense; the proof
does not assert that its ideal has one generator on all of X.

## Explicit computation of the quotient

Let B=k[X]. The following three elements of B are G-invariant:

    a=x^2,             v=v,             d=xu^2-v^2.

Multiplying the last two equations of (1) by u and v gives

    (x^2-1)u^2 = yuv = xv^2.

Consequently

    xd=u^2,            (a-1)d=v^2.                         (2)

We prove that the entire invariant ring is

    B^G = k[a,v,d] / (v^2-(a-1)d).                        (3)

First work on U1, equivalently invert the invariant a. Its coordinate ring is

    B_a = k[x,x^-1,y,u] / (y^2-x^3+x).

A k-basis consists of the Laurent monomials x^s y^b u^c with s in Z,
b in {0,1} and c>=0. Sigma multiplies such a basis element by
i^(2s+b+c). Therefore the invariant subspace has exactly the basis elements
with 2s+b+c divisible by four. On this open set,

    v=yu/x,             d=u^2/x.

If b=0, invariance forces c=2h and s+h even, and

    x^s u^(2h) = a^((s+h)/2) d^h.

If b=1, invariance forces c=2h+1 and s+h+1 even, and

    x^s y u^(2h+1) = a^((s+h+1)/2) v d^h.

This proves generation of (B_a)^G by a,a^-1,v,d. There are no additional
relations: the ring on the right of (3), after inverting a, is free with
basis {1,v} over k[a,a^-1,d], because its defining relation is monic in v.
Before localization it is likewise free over k[a,d], so multiplication by
a is injective and no relation can be hidden by this localization.
Its corresponding monomials map to

    a^r d^h       -> x^(2r-h) u^(2h),
    a^r v d^h     -> x^(2r-h-1) y u^(2h+1).

These are distinct members of the displayed basis of B_a and hence are
linearly independent. This proves (3) after inverting a, including
injectivity of the proposed presentation.

Next invert a-1. The second chart gives

    B_(a-1) = (k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3+x))_(x^2-1)[v],

and v is fixed. In the curve coordinate ring, the basis monomials x^s
and x^s y for s>=0 have sigma-weights 2s and 2s+1. Thus its invariant ring
is k[x^2]=k[a]. Invariants commute with localization at the invariant a-1,
so

    (B_(a-1))^G = k[a,(a-1)^-1,v].

This is exactly the localization of the proposed ring (3), since there
d=v^2/(a-1). The two invariant principal opens cover: a and a-1 generate
the unit ideal. Thus the localized identifications glue to (3).
Equivalently, an invariant b belongs to the proposed subring after either
localization; some powers a^r b and (a-1)^s b lie in it. The ideals generated
by those two powers are comaximal, proving b lies in that subring already.

For an affine variety acted on by a finite group, the affine quotient is
Spec(B^G). The basic existence here also follows from integrality of each
element over the invariant ring via its finite orbit polynomial. We have
therefore identified the actual quotient, not merely a map to a candidate
invariant hypersurface.

## A singular point of the quotient

At the quotient point

    (a,v,d)=(1,0,0),

put A=a-1. The local equation becomes

    v^2=Ad.

This local ring has Krull dimension two and cotangent-space dimension
three: its defining equation has no nonzero linear term in A,v,d. It is
therefore not regular. Indeed this is the unique singular point of (3),
since the three partial derivatives are -d, 2v and -(a-1).

The point comes from the orbit

    {(1,0,0,0), (-1,0,0,0)}.

Each of these two points has stabilizer <sigma^2> of order two, rather
than the whole G. In the smooth coordinates (y,u) near either point,
sigma^2 acts by (y,u) -> (-y,-u), which also explains the quadratic cone.
Thus smoothness of the full fixed divisor does not control all stabilizers
of a cyclic group of composite order. All assumptions printed in 14.72
hold, but X/G is singular.

## Checks, prior context and scope

The proof above uses explicit rings and charts. It does not depend on
classification of quotient singularities or a computer calculation.
`scripts/verify_14_72.py` checks exact Laurent normal forms, 1,661 invariant
monomials, 850 distinct reverse-basis images and the global invariant
identities. `scripts/verify_14_72.g` independently checks all surface points,
the scheme's Jacobian ranks, full and proper stabilizers, separation of
orbits by the proposed global invariants, and the quotient gradient over
five finite fields containing a fourth root of unity. These are controls;
the characteristic-zero assertion rests on the all-degree proof above.

For relevant prior context, Kiraly--Lutkebohmert,
[*Group actions of prime order on local normal rings*](https://doi.org/10.2140/ant.2013.7.63),
Algebra and Number Theory 7 (2013), 63--74, Theorem 2, gives a positive
regularity criterion for a local action of **prime** order with principal
augmentation ideal. Its main theorem statement and introductory scope
were read; its full proof was not audited or used here. It does not concern
the order-four action and proper stabilizers used above. The global fixed
scheme is not the union of fixed schemes of all nonidentity elements.
