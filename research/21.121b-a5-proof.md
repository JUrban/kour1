# A nonsoluble example with exact 2-Jordan exponent log(60)/log(4)

12 September 2026. This is an additional example for 21.121(b), not a
solution of its universal bound. It adds no complete candidate. No
priority or outside acceptance is claimed.

Let S=A5 and let Gamma be the restricted direct product of countably
many copies of S. Thus each element has only finitely many nonidentity
coordinates. Put

    rho = log(60)/log(4).

**Theorem.** Gamma has 2-Jordan exponent exactly rho, and the bound at
rho is attained with constant J=1. In particular 2<rho<3. Gamma is
locally finite and is not virtually soluble.

We use the bound for finite soluble groups proved in
[the soluble-case proof](21.121b-soluble-proof.md): if their odd-order
subgroups are abelian, they have a normal abelian odd-order subgroup
of index at most the square of the Sylow 2-order. That proof includes
the characteristic-subgroup argument and its classical inputs.

## 1. Elementary properties of A5

The nonidentity conjugacy classes of A5 have sizes 15,20,12,12:
double transpositions, 3-cycles, and the two classes of 5-cycles.
These follow by counting cycle types and their centralizers (of orders
4,3,5 respectively). A union of the identity class with a proper
selection of these four classes never has order a proper divisor of
60. Therefore A5 is simple. It is nonabelian, so it is perfect.

A proper subgroup cannot have order 30,20, or 15: its coset action
would embed the simple group A5 in S2, S3, or S4, respectively, which
is impossible by orders. Lagrange's theorem leaves only orders
1,2,3,4,5,6,10,12. All groups of these orders are soluble. For order
12, either the Sylow 3-subgroup is normal, or there are four of them.
In the latter case their eight nonidentity elements leave just three
nonidentity elements for any Sylow 2-subgroup, making that subgroup
unique and normal. The quotient then has order three. The order-six
and order-ten cases have a normal Sylow subgroup of odd prime order;
the remaining cases are immediate. Thus every proper subgroup of S
is soluble and has order at most 12. Every odd-order subgroup of S
has order 1,3, or 5, and is cyclic.

## 2. Subdirect products of a simple group

For any fixed finite nonabelian simple S, a subgroup T of S^m which
projects onto every coordinate is isomorphic to S^r for some r. More
precisely, its factors are diagonal copies on disjoint nonempty sets
of coordinates, possibly with automorphisms twisting the coordinates.

Induct on m. Project T onto the first m-1 coordinates; by induction
the image is S^r. The kernel lies in the last S coordinate and is
normal in that S because the last projection is onto. Simplicity
makes it either S or trivial. In the first case T is the direct
product of its projected image and the last S. In the second, T is
the graph of a surjective homomorphism S^r -> S. The images of the
r direct factors are commuting normal subgroups of S. Exactly one
can be S: two such images would make S abelian, while at least one
must be nontrivial. Its map S -> S is an automorphism, and all the
other factor images are trivial. This gives the asserted diagonal
description. The empty set of coordinates gives the trivial group.

## 3. Every subgroup of A5^n

Let H <= S^n. Separate the coordinates according as the projection
of H is all of S or a proper subgroup. The projection T on the first
set is subdirect, hence T is isomorphic to S^r by Section 2. The
projection B on the second set is soluble, since it lies in a finite
direct product of soluble proper subgroups of S. H embeds in T x B
and projects onto both factors.

In fact H=T x B. To see this without an implicit splitting assumption,
put N_T={t:(t,1) in H} and N_B={b:(1,b) in H}. They are normal in their
respective factors. Matching coordinates of elements of H gives an
isomorphism T/N_T -> B/N_B: surjectivity makes the matching possible,
and the definitions of the kernels make it well-defined and injective.
The first quotient is perfect and the second is soluble. A perfect
soluble group is trivial, by its derived series. Thus N_T=T and N_B=B,
as required.

Every odd-order subgroup of B is abelian: its coordinate projections
are odd-order subgroups of S, hence cyclic by Section 1, and the
coordinate map embeds it in their abelian product. Apply the soluble
bound with J=1 to obtain D normal abelian of odd order in B with

    [B:D] <= |B_(2)|^2.

Under H=T x B, the subgroup 1 x D is normal abelian of odd order.
Writing b=|B_(2)| and using |T|=60^r and |T_(2)|=4^r gives

    [H:1 x D] <= 60^r b^2
               <= (4^r b)^rho = |H_(2)|^rho.             (1)

Here b>=1 and rho>2 because 60>4^2. Also rho<3 because 60<4^3.
This controls every subgroup, including all diagonal subgroups.

## 4. The infinite group and exactness

Every finite set of elements of Gamma is supported on finitely many
coordinates and hence generates a finite group. In particular every
finite subgroup lies in S^n for some n. Equation (1) proves the
2-Jordan bound with constant one at exponent rho.

The embedded full powers S^r have no nontrivial abelian normal
subgroup: every coordinate image of such a subgroup would be abelian
and normal in the nonabelian simple S, and therefore trivial. Their
minimum normal abelian odd-order index is exactly 60^r. For any e<rho,

    60^r/(4^r)^e = (60/4^e)^r

is unbounded. Thus no smaller exponent works, proving exactness and
attainment at rho.

Finally, a soluble subgroup of S^r projects into a proper subgroup
of each S, so has order at most 12^r and index at least 5^r. If Gamma
had a soluble subgroup of fixed finite index d, its intersection
with each S^r would have index at most d, contradicting 5^r>d for
large r. The example therefore has no bounded soluble index.

## Scope and source check

The structural arguments above are elementary forms of the standard
subdirect-simple-group lemma and Goursat's lemma, with their proofs
included. The soluble bound is the earlier deduction in this project.
The definition of p-Jordan agrees with Fei Hu's
[arXiv:1804.10946v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10946v2), Definition 1.6,
as recorded in [the source audit](21.121b-source-audit.md).

Additional searches combining “Jordan exponent” with “alternating,”
and “p-Jordan” with “direct product” or “A5,” located no exact prior
statement of this example. Those bounded searches are not a priority
certificate. In particular the finite-product lemma in Hu does not
by itself give a uniform exponent for arbitrarily many factors; the
subgroup decomposition and (1) supply that step here.

This example is below exponent three. It neither proves nor refutes
the upper bound in the unrestricted 21.121(b).
