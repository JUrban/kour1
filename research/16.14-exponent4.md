# Problem 16.14: the exponent-four case

Status: proved elementary subclass, not a full solution. No novelty
claim is made. The unrestricted finite 2-group question remains open
in this project.

Suppose G is a finite group of exponent dividing four, and every
involution is central. Write W=Omega_1(G). Since all involutions
are central, W is elementary abelian and consists exactly of the
elements whose squares are one. Put w=dim_F2(W). The involutions
of Z(G) are precisely W, so rank(Z(G))=w.

Every square in G belongs to W. Thus V=G/W has exponent dividing
two and is elementary abelian. In particular G has class at most
two, and the square subgroup P=G^2=Phi(G) is contained in W.
Put n=dim(V) and f=dim(P). Then

    d(G)=log_2 |G/P|=n+w-f.

Squaring induces a well-defined map

    q:V -> P,          q(gW)=g^2.

It is well-defined because W is central of exponent two. Its only
zero is the zero vector: if g^2=1, then g belongs to W. Since G has
class at most two, the identity (gh)^2=g^2 h^2 [h,g] shows that q
has quadratic coordinate functions over F_2. More explicitly, choose
lifts g_1,...,g_n of a basis of V. The square of their binary ordered
product is the product of g_i^2 to the corresponding linear powers
and [g_j,g_i] to the corresponding quadratic powers. All these
elements lie in P. Hence q has f coordinates, each of degree at
most two in n binary variables.

An elementary parity argument gives n<=2f. Indeed, if n>2f, then
the parity of the number of simultaneous zeros of the coordinate
polynomials q_1,...,q_f is

    sum_(v in F_2^n) product_(j=1)^f (1+q_j(v)).

The product has degree at most 2f<n. The sum of every monomial
of degree less than n over F_2^n is zero: at least one variable is
absent, so values occur in equal pairs. Thus the number of zeros
would be even. But q has exactly one zero, a contradiction.

Consequently

    d(G)=n+w-f <= w+f <= 2w = 2 rank(Z(G)),

as desired. If n=f=0, the same final inequality holds directly.
The argument includes arbitrary central direct factors; the distinction
between W and P is what accounts for them in the generator count.

This proof is a special instance of the usual polynomial zero-count
parity argument, written here explicitly. It imposes exponent dividing
four; no reduction from larger exponent has been established.
