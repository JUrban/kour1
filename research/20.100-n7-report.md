# 20.100: the all-group case n=7

**Result.** If a group G has no elements of orders 2 through 8, every
seven-element subset has an ordering a1,...,a7 for which
a1,a2^2,...,a7^7 are pairwise distinct. G can be infinite and nonabelian,
and the subset can contain the identity.

This is a completed computational partial result. Together with the
previous certificates it establishes n=4,5,6,7 for all eligible groups.
The conjecture for arbitrary n remains unresolved. It adds no complete
Notebook-problem candidate: the portfolio remains at 43 candidates, all
pending priority checks and outside review. No outside reviewer has
examined this result. In this report, “independent checker” refers to
separate software using different mathematical operations from the
producer; it does not mean an independent human author or reviewer.

## Mathematical argument

The complete structural proofs appear in `20.100-reduction.md`.
For clarity, their role in the n=7 conclusion is as follows.

1. Any finite list of infinite-order elements admits positive lengths
   such that a^i=b^j, for positive i,j, implies i*length(a)=j*length(b).
   In each commensurability class of cyclic subgroups choose a common
   element c=a^k and take length(a)=1/|k|. Sorting lengths and assigning
   increasing exponents gives distinct powers. This uses neither
   commutativity nor unique roots.
2. For a finite-order element in an eligible group, each exponent from
   1 through 7 is coprime to its order. Its relevant powers therefore
   generate the same cyclic subgroup. Separate cyclic coordinates model
   exactly the finite-power equalities among a chosen seven-element set.
   Replace its infinite-order elements by distinct nonidentity elements
   of a separate cyclic group of prime order greater than 8. A good
   exponent assignment in this finite abelian model lifts back: retain
   the finite assignments and use step 1 on the remaining exponents.
   Thus the finite abelian case suffices for all groups.
3. Every eligible finite abelian group is a module over
   S=Z[1/210], since 2,3,5,7 act invertibly. A certificate node gives
   relations R on seven distinguished variables. Its assertion is that
   no tuple of distinct elements satisfying R can have a collision for
   every permutation of the exponents 1,...,7.
4. A leaf proves that R forces two variables equal. A branch chooses one
   exponent permutation and treats all 21 possible collision pairs.
   Each case points to an earlier node and gives a bijective variable
   relabelling. Every relation at that child must follow from R and the
   chosen collision. These implications suffice; the child's claimed
   presentation need not be an exact quotient or a canonical form.
5. Node identifiers are strictly consecutive, and each child is earlier
   than its parent. Induction over identifiers therefore proves every
   node. The last node has an empty presentation, so it excludes every
   counterexample in every S-module. Step 2 gives the claimed group result.

The integer-matrix verifier tests localized row membership independently
of the producer's weighted-component and gcd operations. For every
membership computation it checks U M V=D, the unimodularity of U and V,
and diagonality of D. It strips only the inverted primes from nonzero
diagonal entries, tests the resulting divisibility conditions, and
requires all free coordinates to vanish.

At n=7 each worker checks nodes with (identifier-1) mod 6 equal to its
index. Every worker reads all literal states, so it can test implications
to children assigned elsewhere. Such a child is justified by its own
worker. All six index classes together cover every node exactly once.
An individual `PASS_SHARD` is therefore insufficient; the completed
partition and the decreasing child identifiers justify the induction.

The finite counterexample bound also proved in the reduction note is
11*7^6=1,294,139. This is an independent structural bound. The certificate
does not enumerate groups or subsets through that order.
