# A negative answer to Problem 17.39

September 12, 2026. Complete candidate proof; independent outside review
and priority assessment remain pending.

For each odd prime p and each integer d >= 1, we construct a finite
supersoluble metabelian group G of order 2p^(2d+1) with trivial hypercenter
for which the least number of system normalizers with trivial intersection
is exactly d+1. Consequently there is no uniform integer in Problem 17.39.
It already suffices to fix p=3 and let d grow.

## 1. The group and its center

Write V=F_p^d, with dot product u.v. On P=V x V x F_p put

    (u,v,z)(a,b,c) = (u+a, v+b, z+c+u.b).

This is the matrix group

             [ 1  u  z ]
    M(u,v,z)=[ 0  I  v ],
             [ 0  0  1 ]

where u is a row and v a column. Its identity is (0,0,0), and

    (u,v,z)^(-1) = (-u,-v,-z+u.v).

The map alpha(u,v,z)=(u,-v,-z) is an automorphism of order two:
it preserves the displayed multiplication and its square is the identity.
Let G=P semidirect <t>, where t has order two and x^t=alpha(x).
Equivalently adjoin diag(1,I,-1) to the matrices above. In particular,
|G|=2p^(2d+1), and P is the normal Sylow p-subgroup.

For x=(u,v,z) and y=(a,b,c), the commutator x^(-1)y^(-1)xy is
(0,0,u.b-a.v). Nondegeneracy of the dot product gives

    Z(P)=P'={ (0,0,z): z in F_p }=:Z.

Every nonidentity element of Z is inverted by t, since p is odd.
A central element of G lying in P must therefore be the identity.
An element outside P acts on Z by inversion (P centralizes Z), so it
cannot be central either. Hence Z(G)=1. The upper central series stays
trivial once its first term is trivial, and thus Z_infinity(G)=1.

The subgroup W={ (0,v,z): v in V, z in F_p } is abelian and normal
in G. The quotient G/W is abelian, so G is metabelian and soluble.
In fact, in G/Z the two copies of V are abelian, and t fixes the first
and negates the second. A flag formed by their coordinate subspaces is
invariant under G/Z. Its preimage, preceded by 1<Z and followed by G,
is a normal series with all factors cyclic of prime order. Thus G is
supersoluble as well.

## 2. All system normalizers

A Sylow system consists of one Sylow subgroup for each prime dividing
|G|, with its members pairwise permuting. The system normalizer is the
intersection of their normalizers. Since P is normal, {P,<t>} is a Sylow
system. Its system normalizer is

    D=N_G(P) intersect N_G(<t>)=N_G(<t>)=C_G(t).

The last equality holds because a group of order two has only one
nonidentity element. Comparing (u,v,z) with its image under alpha gives

    U=C_P(t)={ (u,0,0): u in V },
    D=U x <t>,             |D|=2p^d.

Every Sylow system of G consists of P and a Sylow 2-subgroup. Sylow
conjugacy therefore says that its normalizer is a conjugate of D.
Conversely every conjugate of D is such a system normalizer. Moreover,
G=DP, so all conjugates can be written D^g with g in P.

We use x^g=g^(-1)xg. Direct multiplication, for g=(a,b,c) in P, gives

    (u,0,0)^g=(u,0,u.b).                              (1)

Thus the Sylow p-subgroup U^g of D^g is the graph of the linear
functional u -> u.b. Its intersection with P is exactly this graph,
because D^g has p-part p^d and image <t> in G/P.

For completeness the entire conjugate can be recorded without implicit
notation. Represent an element of G as (u,v,z,e) meaning M(u,v,z)t^e,
where e=0 or 1. If q=c-a.b, then

    D^g = D_(b,q)
        = { (u,0,u.b,0): u in V }
          union { (u,-2b,-2q-u.b,1): u in V }.       (2)

To check the second set, multiply g^(-1)(u,0,0)t g and collect t on
the right. Its P-coordinate is (u,-2b,-2c+2a.b-u.b), as asserted.
Every b,q occurs by taking g=(0,b,q). Distinct pairs give distinct
subgroups: the e=1 elements determine b from their v-coordinate and
then determine q from their z-coordinate when u=0. Consequently G has
exactly p^(d+1) system normalizers.

## 3. The exact minimum

Take any k system normalizers D_(b_i,q_i), i=1,...,k. Their intersection
with P consists of

    { (u,0,u.b_1,0):
      u.(b_i-b_1)=0 for every i=2,...,k }.           (3)

The homogeneous system here has at most k-1 independent equations on
the d-dimensional space V. Hence it has dimension at least d-k+1.
If k<=d, its dimension is positive, so the intersection is nontrivial.
This covers arbitrary conjugates, including repeated ones; it is not
just a bound along one chosen sequence of intersections.

For the reverse inequality, take D=D_(0,0), and for each standard basis
vector e_i of V take g_i=(0,e_i,0) and D^g_i=D_(e_i,0). Equation (3)
for these d+1 groups imposes u.e_i=0 for all i, so their common part
in P is the identity. There is no common element outside P already
between D and D^g_1: formula (2) assigns their e=1 elements respective
v-coordinates 0 and -2e_1, which are distinct. Their full intersection
is therefore trivial. This proves that the exact minimum is d+1.

More explicitly, after the initial D and j of the displayed conjugates,
the intersection has order p^(d-j), for 1<=j<=d. Its initial order
before any further conjugate is 2p^d. These orders also explain the
finite verification outputs.

Given any proposed positive uniform n, take d=n and p=3. Every
intersection of n system normalizers in this G is nontrivial, whereas
the hypercenter is trivial. This is the required negative answer.

## 4. Why the prior Frattini hypothesis matters

We also prove Phi(G)=Z, of order p. This is not needed for the
counterexample but locates it relative to the published positive case.

First Phi(P)=P'P^p=Z: the commutator calculation gives P'=Z, and the
power formula

    (u,v,z)^m=(mu,mv,mz+binomial(m,2)u.v)

gives P^p=1 since p is odd. The finite p-group Frattini formula is the
only standard fact used here. Also Phi(P)<=Phi(G): otherwise some
maximal subgroup M of G misses Phi(P). This latter subgroup is normal
in G, so G=M Phi(P), whence

    P=(P intersect M) Phi(P)

by the modular law. The defining nongenerator property of Phi(P)
forces P=P intersect M, a contradiction to Phi(P) not being contained
in M. Thus Z<=Phi(G).

On the other hand,

    G/Z = V x (V semidirect <t>),

where t negates the second V. This quotient has trivial Frattini
subgroup. Indeed, each coordinate hyperplane in the first V, times the
whole second factor, is a maximal subgroup of prime index p; each
coordinate hyperplane in the second V, extended by t and the whole
first V, is also maximal of index p. Finally V x V is maximal of index
two. The intersection of these displayed maximal subgroups is trivial.
Pulling them back to G gives Phi(G)<=Z, as required.

Thus even restricting the Frattini subgroup to fixed order three does
not give a uniform bound: use p=3 throughout this family. The positive
result for Phi(G)=1 does not apply to these groups.

## 5. Logical and computational scope

The proof above is independent of GAP and of any finite catalogue.
Its ingredients are explicit matrix multiplication, elementary linear
algebra, Sylow conjugacy, and the definitions of the center and system
normalizer. The additional Frattini calculation is separated because
it is not an input to the negative answer.

The native and independent verifiers check seven finite cases and
enumerate every possible intersection state after conjugating the first
normalizer to D. They confirm the exact minimum, not merely an upper
bound furnished by a successful witness. The proof handles all d and
all odd primes. See the accompanying report and source audit for the
precise computational and priority limits.
