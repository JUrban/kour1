# Problem 15.65: the unitary cyclic limit is not rational

Date: 11 September 2026.
Status: complete candidate for the unitary cyclic component; internal audit
and exact controls pass. Outside review and novelty checking remain pending.

## Result and precise scope

Let U(n,q) be the full group of matrices over F_(q^2) preserving a
nondegenerate Hermitian form, and let c_U(n,q) be the proportion whose
minimal polynomial equals their characteristic polynomial. Then

    C(U;q) = lim_(n->infinity) c_U(n,q)

is not a rational function of q. In fact, no rational function with complex
coefficients agrees with these values on all prime powers, or even on all
powers of any one fixed prime.

This answers the unitary cyclic part of Problem 15.65 negatively, and hence
disproves the proposed general rationality assertion. It does not classify
all the other limits in that problem. The definitions of cyclic matrices
in the Notebook and the source agree. The Notebook's definition of
"separable" uses the minimal polynomial, whereas our source uses the
characteristic polynomial; no change to that definition is used here.

## 1. The published product

We import the first formula of Theorem 2.1.9, printed p.39, in
J. Fulman, P. M. Neumann and C. E. Praeger, *A generating function approach
to the enumeration of matrices in classical groups over finite fields*,
Memoirs of the AMS 176 (2005), no.830, vi+90,
[DOI 10.1090/memo/0830](https://doi.org/10.1090/memo/0830).
The author-hosted PDF is cached in `references/cache/` and available at
https://dornsife.usc.edu/jason-fulman/wp-content/uploads/sites/224/2023/06/chapter.pdf.
It states that the limit exists and equals

    (1+q^(-1))
    * product_(d odd >=1) (1-1/[q^d(q^d+1)])^N_d(q)
    * product_(d>=1) (1+1/[q^(2d)(q^(2d)-1)])^M_d(q).       (1)

Here Lemma 1.3.12, p.23, gives

    N_d(q) = (1/d) sum_(e|d) mu(e)(q^(d/e)+1),  d odd;

    M_1(q) = (q^2-q-2)/2;
    M_d(q) = (1/(2d)) sum_(e|d) mu(e)(q^(2d/e)-q^(d/e)),
                                                      d>1 odd;
    M_d(q) = (1/(2d)) sum_(e|d) mu(e)q^(2d/e), d even.

These are polynomial expressions in q; their values at prime powers count
self-conjugate irreducible polynomials and unordered conjugate pairs.
The proof below uses their explicit formulas, not a notion of a finite
field of nonintegral or negative order.

## 2. An analytic function near t=0

Set t=1/q and define rational functions

    A(u) = 1-u^2/(1+u) = (1+u-u^2)/(1+u),
    B(v) = 1+v^2/(1-v) = (1-v+v^2)/(1-v).

Formula (1) is the value at t=1/q of the analytic germ

    F(t) = (1+t) exp(sum_(d odd>=1) N_d(1/t) log A(t^d)
                      + sum_(d>=1) M_d(1/t) log B(t^(2d))),              (2)

where each logarithm is the branch vanishing at zero. We now justify both
the germ and a larger domain for the product excluding d=1 in the first
sum. Put R=3/4 and

    H(t) = exp(sum_(d odd>=3) N_d(1/t) log A(t^d)
                 + sum_(d>=1) M_d(1/t) log B(t^(2d))).                  (3)

**Claim.** H is holomorphic and nowhere zero on |t|<R, including t=0,
and H(0)=1.

Here are uniform estimates that establish the claim without merely formal
manipulation. For 0<|t|<1, the displayed polynomial-count formulas and the
bound on the number of divisors by d give

    |N_d(1/t)| <= 2 |t|^(-d),
    |M_d(1/t)| <= 2 |t|^(-2d).                                      (4)

The exceptional formula for M_1 satisfies the second estimate as well.
For d>=3 and |t|<=R,

    |t^(2d)/(1+t^d)| <= R^6/(1-R^3) = 729/2368 < 1.

For d>=1 and |t|<=R,

    |t^(4d)/(1-t^(2d))| <= R^4/(1-R^2) = 81/112 < 1.

The convergent series for log(1+z) therefore define the logarithms in (3)
on this whole disc. There are constants K_A,K_B, independent of d, with

    |log A(t^d)| <= K_A |t|^(2d),
    |log B(t^(2d))| <= K_B |t|^(4d).                                 (5)

For example, obtain these bounds from
|log(1+z)| <= |z|/(1-|z|), with the two preceding uniform bounds and
|1+t^d|>=1-R^3 or |1-t^(2d)|>=1-R^2, respectively.
Each product of a count polynomial and its logarithm has a removable
singularity at zero: the pole order is at most d or 2d, whereas the
logarithm has a zero of order at least 2d or 4d. Combining (4)--(5)
bounds its extension by 2K_A |t|^d or 2K_B |t|^(2d). Thus both series
in (3) converge uniformly on each compact subdisc (indeed the same
majorants work up to R). Their sum is holomorphic and vanishes at zero.
Its exponential proves the claim.

The omitted factor also defines a holomorphic germ at zero, since
log A(t) vanishes to order two. Because N_1(1/t)=1+1/t, equation (2)
can now be written

    F(t) = (1+t) H(t) exp((1+1/t) log A(t)),    F(0)=1.                (6)

For positive t=1/q, q a prime power, the logarithms are the ordinary
real logarithms of positive factors, so (1) gives exactly

    F(1/q) = C(U;q).                                                 (7)

## 3. A nonintegral local exponent

Let

    alpha=(1-sqrt(5))/2,     -3/4 < alpha < -1/2.

The numerator 1+t-t^2 of A(t) has a simple zero at alpha and its other
zero lies outside the unit disc. Its denominator is nonzero at alpha.
For alpha<t<0 the value A(t) is positive. Formula (6), with the real
logarithm on this interval, is analytic continuation of the germ at zero.
All the logarithms defining H are real on the interval, so H(alpha)>0.
In particular, H neither cancels nor modifies the order of the first
factor at alpha.

Write x=t-alpha>0. There is a constant

    K=A'(alpha)=sqrt(5)/(1+alpha)>0

such that A(alpha+x)=Kx(1+O(x)). The quadratic relation
alpha^2=alpha+1 gives

    1+1/alpha=alpha.

Since (1+1/(alpha+x))-alpha=O(x) and x log x -> 0, equation (6) gives

    lim_(x->0+) F(alpha+x)/x^alpha
         = (1+alpha) H(alpha) K^alpha,                               (8)

a finite, strictly positive number. Thus this analytic continuation has
local exponent alpha, which is irrational (in particular not an integer).

A nonzero rational function has an integer order at each finite point:
it is x^m times a holomorphic function nonzero at that point, for an
integer m. Such a function cannot satisfy (8), because x^(m-alpha)
has no finite nonzero limit when m is an integer. Therefore F cannot
be a rational germ.

## 4. Why the restriction to field sizes does not evade the contradiction

Suppose a rational function Q(q) agrees with C(U;q) on all prime powers.
Then P(t)=Q(1/t) is rational, and P(2^(-k))=F(2^(-k)) for every k>=1.
Since F(0)=1, P cannot have a pole at zero, and its removable value there
is one. The identity theorem applied near zero now gives P=F as analytic
germs. Uniqueness of analytic continuation then identifies P with (6)
along alpha<t<0. A pole of P at an interior point of this interval would
already contradict the holomorphy of (6) there. At its endpoint alpha,
(8) contradicts the integer order required of P. This proves the result.

The same argument uses p^(-k) for any fixed prime p, so even agreement
only on all powers of one prime is impossible. The argument does not
claim that an individual number C(U;q) is irrational.

## 5. Attribution and proof dependence

The enumeration and infinite-product formula are prior work of
Fulman--Neumann--Praeger. Their chapter treats the rationality question
as open at the time of writing. The proposed contribution here is the
analytic continuation in the field-size variable, isolation of its
first singular factor, and proof that no rational function can interpolate
the prime-power values. This application is a candidate for independent
review and a fuller priority search, not an assertion of established
novelty.

No finite computation is used to establish (8) or nonrationality. Exact
coefficient and finite-group checks, and the source-reading limits, are
recorded separately.
