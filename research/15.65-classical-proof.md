# Problem 15.65: nonrationality of all the stated classical limits

11 September 2026. Complete candidate extension, internally audited;
independent review and priority checks remain pending. The original
unitary cyclic packet is frozen separately. No additional candidate number.

## The result

For each of the full classical families

    U(n,q), Sp(2m,q), O(2m+1,q), O+(2m,q), O-(2m,q),

the limiting proportion of cyclic matrices and the limiting proportion
of matrices with squarefree minimal polynomial are not rational functions
of q. For the symplectic and orthogonal families this holds separately
on even prime powers and on odd prime powers. It also holds for the
limiting proportions with squarefree characteristic polynomial.

Thus all the limits asked about in Problem15.65 have negative answers,
both with its literal definition of separable (squarefree MINIMAL
polynomial) and with the source's definition (squarefree CHARACTERISTIC
polynomial). We distinguish these two functions throughout. The result
concerns rational functions of field size, not rationality of any one
individual numerical probability.

All existence and enumeration formulas below are imported from J. Fulman,
P. M. Neumann and C. E. Praeger, *A generating function approach to the
enumeration of matrices in classical groups over finite fields*, Memoirs
AMS 176 (2005), no. 830, vi+90, DOI 10.1090/memo/0830 (FNP).
The [author-hosted text](https://dornsife.usc.edu/jason-fulman/wp-content/uploads/sites/224/2023/06/chapter.pdf)
is retained in the source packet. The nonrationality arguments are given
here. In FNP's unitary notation the underlying field has q^2 elements;
nonrationality in q also excludes rationality in that field size q^2.

## 1. Polynomial counts, with characteristic fixed

Write t=1/q. In the symplectic and orthogonal cases fix e=1 (even q)
or e=2 (odd q). FNP Lemma1.3.16 gives the polynomials

    J_d(q) = N*(q;2d)
           = (1/(2d)) sum_(a|d, a odd) mu(a)(q^(d/a)+1-e),

    K_1(q) = (q-e-1)/2,
    K_d(q) = (1/2) N(q;d),                         d>1 odd,
    K_d(q) = (1/2)(N(q;d)-N*(q;d)),                d even,

where, for d>1,

    N(q;d)=(1/d) sum_(a|d) mu(a)q^(d/a),

and N*(q;d)=J_(d/2)(q) when d is even. In particular,

    J_1(1/t) = (1/t+1-e)/2.                         (9)

For unitary groups keep the polynomials N_d,M_d from section1 of
`15.65-proof.md`; their relevant first exponent is

    N_1(1/t)=1+1/t.                                (10)

The displayed formulas, |mu(a)|<=1 and the bound on the number of
divisors show, for 0<|t|<1, that there is an absolute constant C with

    |J_d(1/t)|+|K_d(1/t)| <= C |t|^(-d).             (11)

For instance C=8 works for both values of e, including the exceptional
degree-one formulas. The analogous bounds for N_d and M_d, with pole
orders d and2d, were proved in the original unitary packet.

Whenever f(u) is holomorphic and nonzero on a disc about zero,
f(0)=1 and log f(u)=O(u^2), these estimates imply normal convergence
near t=0 of sums of count polynomials times log f(t^d). The poles at
zero are removable. On a larger domain we can omit finitely many
first factors and use the same argument, since the higher powers t^d
remain in a fixed smaller zero-free disc. These facts will be checked
at each use below; no formal infinite product alone is used to assert
continuation or nonvanishing.

## 2. Local branching lemma

Suppose a(t) is holomorphic near a nonzero point b and has a zero there
of multiplicity h>=1. Suppose P(t) is holomorphic and nonconstant near
b, and K(t) is a nonzero single-valued meromorphic germ near b. A branch
of

    K(t) exp(P(t) log a(t))                         (12)

cannot be the continuation of a rational germ.

To prove this, write a(t)=(t-b)^h v(t) with v holomorphic and nonzero.
After shrinking the disc, v has a holomorphic logarithm and K has no
zeros or poles in the punctured disc. Continuing once around b changes
(12) by the factor

    exp(2*pi*i*h*P(t)).                             (13)

A rational function is single-valued, so equality with a rational germ
would require (13) to be identically one on an open set. Differentiating
that identity gives P'(t)=0 there and hence P is constant, a
contradiction. A zero or pole of integer order contributed by K does
not change this argument.

For every function considered below, its analytic germ at t=0 agrees
with the relevant probability at all sufficiently small t=1/q of the
chosen characteristic parity. A putative rational interpolant is
removable at zero, since those values have a finite nonzero limit
there. Agreement on q=2^k for e=1, or q=3^k for e=2, invokes the identity
theorem. In the unitary case either sequence suffices. We may therefore
apply the branching lemma to the continued germ. Possible zeros of a
single-valued prefactor along a continuation path are harmless; they
are ordinary zeros, not branch points.

## 3. All cyclic limits

Put

    A(u)=(1+u-u^2)/(1+u),
    B(u)=(1-u+u^2)/(1-u)=(1+u^3)/(1-u^2).

FNP Theorem2.2.9 gives

    C(Sp;q) = product_(d>=1) A(t^d)^J_d(1/t) B(t^d)^K_d(1/t).        (14)

The first A-factor has its base zero at

    alpha=(1-sqrt(5))/2,

and exponent (9), a nonconstant function of t. Remove this factor.
For |t|<3/4 and d>=2,

    |t^(2d)/(1+t^d)| <= (3/4)^4/(1-(3/4)^2)=81/112<1,

so log A(t^d) is a single-valued holomorphic function. For all d>=1
use log B(t^d)=log(1+t^(3d))-log(1-t^(2d)), which is holomorphic on
|t|<1. Both logarithms vanish to order at least2d; (11) bounds their
products by constants times |t|^d. Thus the remaining logarithmic sum
converges normally on |t|<3/4, extends over zero, and its exponential
is nonvanishing there. The branching lemma proves nonrationality.
Alternatively, the local exponent at alpha is (alpha-e)/2, which is
irrational, so the integer-order argument of the original proof applies.

The unitary cyclic case was proved independently in `15.65-proof.md`.
FNP Theorem2.3.11 gives, with t=1/q,

    C(O odd;q) = (1-t) C(Sp;q),
    C(O+;q) = C(O-;q) = ((1-t)^e+1)/2 * C(Sp;q).                   (15)

These multipliers are nonzero rational functions, separately for fixed
e, so every orthogonal cyclic limit is nonrational as well. The
orthogonal groups here are the full groups specified by FNP; neither
special orthogonal nor projective groups have been substituted.

## 4. Two analytic series for semisimple matrices

Define, for |u|<1,

    U(u) = sum_(m>=0) u^(m^2)/product_(i=1..m)(1-(-u)^i),
    L(u) = sum_(m>=0) u^(m^2)/product_(i=1..m)(1-u^i).              (16)

Empty products are one. These are the field-size continuations of
sum_m 1/|U(m,q)| and sum_m 1/|GL(m,q)|. On every compact subdisc,
the absolute values of the denominator products are bounded below
by product_(i>=1)(1-r^i)>0, r<1. Thus (16) converges normally and is
holomorphic on the unit disc. Both series start 1+u+O(u^2).

We need elementary, explicit zero controls. Put

    a_m(r)=r^(m^2)/product_(i=1..m)(1-r^i),    0<r<1.

For m>=1,

    a_(m+1)(r)/a_m(r) = r^(2m+1)/(1-r^(m+1))
                      <= r^3/(1-r^2).                           (17)

At r=3/7, summing the geometric majorant gives

    sum_(m>=1) a_m(3/7)
        <= (3/4)/(1-27/280)=210/253<1.                            (18)

Consequently both U(u) and L(u) are nonzero for |u|<=3/7, because
the absolute value of their difference from one is less than one.

For negative real u=-r, the U-series is

    U(-r)=1-a_1(r)+a_2(r)-a_3(r)+... .                            (19)

For r<=3/5 the terms a_m(r), m>=1, strictly decrease to zero by
(17). For 0<r<=1/2, a_1(r)<=1 and the alternating tail after it is
positive, so U(-r)>0. At r=1/2 its lower bound from m<=3 is 9/56.
At r=3/5 its upper bound from m<=4 is exactly

    1-a_1+a_2-a_3+a_4 = -1128797279/26656000000 < 0.              (20)

It follows that U has a first negative real zero b in (-3/5,-1/2),
with U(t)>0 for b<t<=0. Existence of a first zero follows by taking
the minimum positive r with U(-r)=0 in the compact interval[1/2,3/5].
It has finite positive multiplicity, since U is holomorphic and U(0)=1.
No assertion of an exact value or simple multiplicity is required.

We also need L(t) to be nonzero on the path to b. For 0<r<=13/20,
write L(-r)=1-b_1+b_2-b_3+... with

    b_m=r^(m^2)/product_(i=1..m)(1-(-r)^i)>0.

The ratio b_(m+1)/b_m is at most r^3/(1-r^2)<1 on this interval.
Thus

    L(-r)>1-b_1=1-r/(1+r)>0.                                    (21)

In particular L is holomorphic and nonzero near the entire real
interval[b,0]. After shrinking to a neighborhood of that interval,
it has a holomorphic logarithm agreeing with its branch at zero.

## 5. The literal S in the Notebook: unitary and symplectic groups

Set

    E(u)=(1-u)U(u),          D(u)=(1-u)L(u).

Both are holomorphic and nonzero on |u|<=3/7 and have expansion
1+O(u^2). FNP Lemma3.1.9(a),(b) identifies these with A_(q,d)(1)
and B_(q,d)(1), respectively, after u=q^(-d). Its Theorem3.1.13 gives

    SS(U;q)=(1+t)
       * product_(d odd>=1) E(t^d)^N_d(1/t)
       * product_(d>=1) D(t^(2d))^M_d(1/t).                      (22)

Here SS means squarefree MINIMAL polynomial, exactly the literal S
in Problem15.65. Remove the d=1 E-factor from (22). Take R=13/20.
Every remaining argument t^d or t^(2d) has modulus at most

    R^2=169/400<3/7.

The logarithms are therefore single-valued and holomorphic, and are
O(t^(2d)) or O(t^(4d)) with uniform constants. The count-polynomial
bounds prove normal convergence and removability at zero. Their
exponential is holomorphic and nonzero on |t|<R. The factor1+t is
nonzero at b. The only branch point needed is the zero of E(t) at b,
with nonconstant exponent1+1/t. The local branching lemma proves
that SS(U;q) is not rational.

For symplectic groups define the single-valued holomorphic function

    P(t)=1+sum_(m>=1) t^(m(2m+1))/product_(i=1..m)(1-t^(2i)).       (23)

It is holomorphic on |t|<1 by the same denominator-product argument
as for (16), and P(0)=1. FNP Theorem3.1.15 states

    SS(Sp;q)=(1-t)^e P(t)^e
       * product_(d>=1) E(t^d)^J_d(1/t) D(t^d)^K_d(1/t).          (24)

The M* exponent here is M*(q;d), as in Theorem3.1.15 and the
definition on p.64. Lemma3.1.9(e) prints M*(q;2d), an inconsistent
index in that intermediate display. Formula(24) uses the theorem's
correct index, also obtained directly by applying Lemma1.3.17(a)
to the product on p.64.

Remove the d=1 E-factor and also separate the d=1 D-factor.
All factors of degrees d>=2 have a normally convergent, zero-free
product on |t|<R, exactly as above using (11). The separated D(t)
is holomorphic and nonzero near[b,0] by(21); therefore
D(t)^K_1(1/t) has a single-valued holomorphic branch there, with a
removable value at zero since log D(t)=O(t^2). The prefactor
(1-t)^e P(t)^e is a single-valued holomorphic function, not identically
zero. Its possible finite-order zeros at b are harmless in the
branching lemma. The exponent of E(t) is the nonconstant function(9).
Hence SS(Sp;q) is nonrational, for each e separately.

## 6. Orthogonal semisimple limits

Define one more single-valued function, holomorphic on |t|<1,

    Q(t)=1+sum_(m>=1) t^(m(2m-1))/product_(i=1..m)(1-t^(2i)).       (25)

Then Q(0)=1. The standard full orthogonal group orders give

    Q(1/q)=1+sum_(m>=1)(1/|O+(2m,q)|+1/|O-(2m,q)|).

Indeed, summing the two reciprocal order formulas combines
(1-t^m)^(-1) and (1+t^m)^(-1) into2/(1-t^(2m)), including the
factor1/2 in each orthogonal order. This holds in both parities.
Thus Q is the field-size continuation of F_+(1) in FNP.

Write

    T_e(t)=product_(d>=1) E(t^d)^J_d(1/t) D(t^d)^K_d(1/t).

The argument just given shows that its local branching around b
comes from E(t)^J_1(1/t), multiplied by a nonzero holomorphic germ.
FNP Theorem3.1.18 gives

    SS(O odd;q)=(1-t)^e Q(t)^(e-1) P(t) T_e(t),
    SS(O+;q)=SS(O-;q)
       =(1-t)^e [Q(t)^e+(e-1)P(t)^2]/2 * T_e(t).                (26)

The multipliers of T_e are single-valued holomorphic functions and
are not identically zero: their values at zero are1 and e/2,
respectively. Each has at most a finite-order zero at b. They cannot
cancel the nonconstant-exponent monodromy, by section2. This proves
nonrationality for all orthogonal semisimple limits, separately for
e=1 and e=2.

## 7. The source's S: squarefree characteristic polynomials

For completeness, the same negative result also holds under FNP's
meaning of separable, although this definition is not needed to answer
the Notebook literally. Put

    V(u)=1-2u^2/(1+u)=(1-u)(1+2u)/(1+u).

FNP Theorems2.1.3 and2.2.3 give

    RS(U;q)=(1+t) product_(d odd>=1) V(t^d)^N_d(1/t),
    RS(Sp;q)=(1-t)^e product_(d>=1) V(t^d)^J_d(1/t).              (27)

The first factor has a simple base zero at -1/2. Every higher-degree
factor is nonzero on |t|<13/20, since |t^d|<169/400<1/2 for d>=2.
Their logarithms vanish to order2d and the polynomial-count bounds
again give a normally convergent, nonvanishing remainder. The first
exponents(9),(10) are nonconstant, even when their values at -1/2
happen to be integers. The branching lemma proves nonrationality.
FNP Theorem2.3.4 gives

    RS(O odd;q)=RS(Sp;q),
    RS(O+;q)=RS(O-;q)=2^(e-2) RS(Sp;q),                          (28)

so these are nonrational too. In particular, an integral value of a
variable exponent at the singular point does not turn the expression
into a meromorphic function; its monodromy still varies with t.

## 8. Scope of the conclusion

The proof uses fixed-parity analytic functions when e occurs. A
single rational formula on all prime powers would restrict to a
rational formula on either parity, which has already been ruled out.
The identity-theorem argument applies just as well to all sufficiently
large powers of any fixed prime. Each field-size germ has a finite
nonzero value at zero (one, or in certain even orthogonal cases1/2).
Thus no pole at zero of a putative rational interpolant is possible.

The source's finite-dimensional variable, used to derive its limits,
is distinct from t, the inverse field size used in this proof. No
claim of an analytic family of groups at complex field sizes is made.
The imported enumeration theorems, their reading limits, exact controls,
and the internal audit of this extension are recorded in
`15.65-classical-review.md`. A discrepancy in one published coefficient
is documented there; no coefficient table is a premise of this proof.
