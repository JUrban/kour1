# Problem 19.56: solubility from restricted coprime commutators

11 September 2026. Complete candidate proof, with internal logical
audit and independent GAP/Python controls. Outside review and broader
priority checking remain pending.

## Statement and conventions

For a finite group G, put

    X(G) = { [x,y] : |x| and |y| are powers of distinct primes },
    [x,y] = x^-1 y^-1 x y,      x^y = y^-1 x y.

Allowing the identity among the inputs or outputs makes no difference.
The orders of the outputs in X(G) need not themselves be prime powers.
Problem 19.56 on printed page 139 asks whether the following condition
implies that G is soluble:

    |ab| >= |a||b| whenever a,b belong to X(G) and gcd(|a|,|b|)=1.    (H)

**Theorem. Yes. In fact it suffices to impose (H) when the two outputs
a and b also have prime-power orders.**

The proof uses the hyperfocal subgroup theorem, Thompson's classification
of minimal simple groups, and the Baer–Suzuki theorem. Those established
theorems are imported explicitly. All deductions specific to the
restricted inputs in this question are supplied below. In particular,
we never assert that (H) passes to quotients.

## 1. An elementary consequence of the order condition

**Normalizer lemma.** Suppose G satisfies the restricted version of (H).
Let a in X(G) have q-power order and normalize a p-subgroup R, where
p != q. Then a centralizes R.

For z in R, b=[a,z] belongs to R. Moreover, it belongs to X(G): its
inputs a and z have coprime prime-power orders. If b is nonidentity,
the condition applies to the two outputs a and b. But

    ab = a[a,z] = a^z,

so |a|=|ab|>=|a||b|, a contradiction. Thus [a,z]=1 for every z in R.

This argument adapts Monakhov's normalizer lemma for ordinary
prime-power-order commutators. The important extra check is that
[a,z] satisfies the question's restriction on its inputs.

## 2. The Sylow generation needed here

Write O^p(G) for the intersection of all normal subgroups with p-group
quotient. This is a residual, not the normal p-core O_p(G).

The finite-group hyperfocal theorem says that, for P in Syl_p(G),

    P intersect O^p(G)
      = < [x,g] : x in Q <= P, g in N_G(Q), gcd(|g|,p)=1 >.         (1)

See Broto–Castellana–Grodal–Levi–Oliver, *Extensions of p-local finite
groups*, Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 359 (2007), 3791–3858, Lemma 2.2,
printed page 3804. Their displayed formula immediately preceding the
lemma is exactly the element-normalizer formulation (1). The theorem
is credited there to Puig, *The hyperfocal subalgebra of a block*,
Invent. Math. 141 (2000), 365–397, Section 1.1.

**Restricted generation lemma.** For every finite G and P in Syl_p(G),

    P intersect O^p(G) = < P intersect X(G) >.                     (2)

Indeed, decompose g in (1) into its commuting primary components
g_1,...,g_k in the cyclic group <g>. Each g_i normalizes Q and has
prime-power order coprime to p. The identity

    [x,uv] = [x,v] [x,u]^v

expresses [x,g] as a product of conjugates of [x,g_i]. Each such
factor belongs to Q, has p-power order, and is in X(G), because
conjugation preserves both input orders. This proves inclusion in
the right side of (2). For the reverse inclusion, consider a member
[u,v] of X(G) in G/O^p(G). At least one of u and v has order coprime
to p and therefore has trivial image in this p-group quotient.
Consequently every member of X(G) lies in O^p(G).

If G is perfect, its p-group quotient G/O^p(G) is also perfect and
therefore trivial. Thus (2) implies

    P = < P intersect X(G) >   whenever G=G'.                     (3)

No closure of X(G) under powers, or unrestricted use of the ordinary
focal theorem, is required.

## 3. A smallest counterexample is a central extension

Assume that a nonsoluble G satisfies the restricted version of (H),
and choose it of least order. The condition passes to subgroups:
their restricted commutators are also restricted commutators of G,
with the same orders. Hence every proper subgroup of G is soluble.
If G'<G, then G' and G/G' are soluble, a contradiction. Thus G=G'.

Let R be the soluble radical. It is proper. If a maximal subgroup M
does not contain R, then G=RM. Both R and M are soluble, and
G/R is a quotient of M, again a contradiction. Therefore R<=Phi(G).
The Frattini subgroup of a finite group is nilpotent: if P is one
of its Sylow subgroups, the Frattini argument gives
G=Phi(G) N_G(P), forcing N_G(P)=G by the definition of Phi(G).
Thus all its Sylow subgroups are normal. In particular Phi(G) is
soluble and lies in R. Hence R=Phi(G). Every proper normal subgroup of G is
soluble and contained in R; therefore S=G/Phi(G) is nonabelian simple.
Every proper subgroup of S has soluble full preimage in G. Thus S
is minimal simple.

Fix a prime p dividing |Phi(G)| and its unique Sylow subgroup R_p.
Since Phi(G) is nilpotent and characteristic, R_p is normal in G.
For any different prime q, a Sylow q-subgroup Q of G is generated
by Q intersect X(G), by (3). Each such generator normalizes R_p
and so centralizes it by the normalizer lemma. Thus Q<=C_G(R_p).
The normal subgroup C_G(R_p) consequently has p-power index in G.
Perfection of G makes this quotient trivial. This holds for every p,
so Phi(G)<=Z(G). The centre is a proper soluble normal subgroup and
therefore is contained in R=Phi(G). We have proved

    Phi(G)=Z(G),     S=G/Z(G) is minimal simple.                  (4)

Only the structure of the quotient is used; it need not satisfy (H).

## 4. A local subgroup in every minimal simple group

**Local lemma.** Every finite minimal nonabelian simple group S has
a nontrivial elementary abelian 2-subgroup V and an odd-prime-order
element ybar which normalizes V and acts nontrivially on V.

Thompson's classification gives the following exhaustive list:

* PSL_2(2^r), r prime;
* PSL_2(3^r), r an odd prime;
* PSL_2(p), p>3 prime and p^2+1 divisible by 5;
* Sz(2^r), r an odd prime;
* PSL_3(3).

The required subgroups can be exhibited in each family.

For PSL_2(q) with q even, use the elementary abelian group of
upper-unitriangular matrices U(c), c in F_q. A diagonal matrix
diag(lambda,lambda^-1), where lambda has prime order dividing q-1,
normalizes this group and scales c by lambda^-2. Since lambda has
odd order greater than one, this action is nontrivial. Here q>=4.

For PSL_2(q) with q odd, we exhibit A4. There are a,b in F_q with
a^2+b^2=-1: the two sets {a^2} and {-1-b^2} each have (q+1)/2
elements and therefore meet. In SL_2(q) put

    i = [[0,1],[-1,0]],        j = [[a,b],[b,-a]],        k=ij.

These matrices satisfy

    i^2=j^2=k^2=-I,    ij=k=-ji.

They generate a quaternion group of order eight. To check that it
does not collapse, i and j are noncentral and do not commute: otherwise
ij=-ij would contradict invertibility in odd characteristic. Its image
modulo {I,-I} is therefore a Klein four group V. The matrices I,i,j,k
are linearly independent: their Gram matrix for the trace pairing
(A,B) -> trace(AB) is diag(2,-2,-2,-2), which is invertible in odd
characteristic.

Set t=(-I+i+j+k)/2. If s=i+j+k, then s^2=-3I, whence
t^2=(-I-s)/2 and t^3=I. Also t has determinant one: its quaternion
norm is (1+1+1+1)/4=1, or directly its product with (-I-s)/2 is I
and that matrix is its 2-by-2 adjugate. Linear independence shows
that t is nonscalar. Direct multiplication gives it=tj, jt=tk,
and kt=ti. Consequently t has order three, its projective image
also has order three, and it cycles the three nonidentity elements
of V. All calculations remain valid in characteristic three; no
division by three is used.

For PSL_3(3), use the determinant-one diagonal matrices with entries
in {1,-1} as V. The permutation matrix of a coordinate 3-cycle has
order three and cycles V's three nonidentity elements. The centre
of SL_3(3) is trivial, so these are already subgroups of PSL_3(3).

For Sz(q), q=2^(2m+1), m>=1, put t=2^(m+1). In its standard
four-dimensional matrix realization, the following matrices belong
to the group for b in F_q and lambda in F_q^*:

    v(b) = [[1,0,0,0], [0,1,0,0], [b,0,1,0], [b^t,b,0,1]],
    d(lambda) = diag(lambda^(t+1),lambda,lambda^-1,lambda^(-t-1)).

This is the b-axis of the standard unipotent subgroup, as in
Bray–Bäärnhielm, *A new method for recognising Suzuki groups*,
arXiv:1706.05697v1, page 1, equations (1)–(3) and the paragraph
defining F and H. Direct multiplication gives

    v(b)v(c)=v(b+c),
    v(b)^d(lambda)=v(lambda^(t+2) b).

The second equality uses t(t+2) congruent to 2t+2 modulo q-1,
since t^2=2q. Thus V={v(b)} is elementary abelian of order q.
Moreover gcd(t+2,q-1)=1: the odd common divisor would also divide
2^m+1; from 2^m=-1 and 2^(2m+1)=1 it would follow that 2=1.
Take lambda of any prime order dividing q-1. Then d(lambda) has
that same odd prime order and acts nontrivially on V. This finishes
all families and proves the local lemma.

## 5. Lift the local subgroup and obtain a forbidden pair

Let pi:G->S be the central quotient in (4), with kernel Z=Z(G).
Choose V and ybar as in the local lemma, and put E=pi^-1(V).
Since E/Z is abelian, E'<=Z<=Z(E); hence E is nilpotent of class
at most two. Its Sylow 2-subgroup P is unique and characteristic,
and pi(P)=V. For the last assertion, decompose any element of E
into its commuting primary components: its odd components have
trivial image in V, so the 2-component has the same image.

Write q for the odd prime |ybar|. There is a q-element y in G
with pi(y)=ybar. For instance, take any lift h, write h=h_q h_(q'),
and note that pi(h_(q'))=1 while pi(h_q)=ybar. The lift y normalizes
E and its characteristic subgroup P. Choose u in P whose image
is moved by ybar. Then

    a=[u,y] is in P intersect X(G),
    abar=pi(a) is a nonidentity member of V.

Thus a has 2-power order, and abar is an involution in S.

Because S is nonabelian simple, O_2(S)=1. The Baer–Suzuki theorem
therefore gives gbar in S such that <abar,abar^gbar> is not a
2-group. Two involutions generate a dihedral group: putting
c0=abar abar^gbar, the element abar inverts c0 and the group is
<c0> semidirect <abar>. Its order is 2|c0|, so |c0| has an odd
prime divisor r. Put c=c0^(|c0|/r). Then c has order r and abar
inverts c. In particular

    Dbar=<abar,c> is dihedral of order 2r.

Let D=pi^-1(Dbar) and L=pi^-1(<c>). The subgroup L is normal in D
and has cyclic quotient L/Z. A group modulo a central subgroup
being cyclic is abelian: every element is a power of one lift times
a central element. Hence L is abelian. Its unique Sylow r-subgroup
R is characteristic in L and normal in D, and pi(R)=<c> by the
same primary-component argument used above.

Now a belongs to D, so it normalizes R. The normalizer lemma makes
a centralize R. Passing this equality to S would make abar
centralize c, whereas it inverts the nonidentity element c of odd
order. This contradiction proves the theorem.

Equivalently, the contradiction produces z in R such that b=[a,z]
is nontrivial. Both a and b are restricted commutators of coprime
prime-power orders, but |ab|=|a|<|a||b|. This records precisely how
the original order condition fails.

## 6. Prior results and the boundary of the claim

Monakhov's *The Nilpotency Criterion for the Derived Subgroup of a
Finite Group*, arXiv:1704.01746v1, Theorem 2, treats ordinary
commutators with prime-power **outputs**. Its normalizer lemma and
minimal-counterexample/central-quotient/dihedral strategy motivated
this argument. That theorem does not impose prime-power coprime
**inputs**, and so does not directly answer 19.56. The generation
lemma above is the needed restricted-input replacement; the local
subgroup argument also supplies a restricted witness explicitly.

Monakhov's *A metanilpotency criterion for a finite solvable group*,
Trudy Inst. Mat. Mekh. UrO RAN 23(4) (2017), 253–256, translated in
Proc. Steklov Inst. Math. 304 suppl. 1 (2019), S141–S143, already
proves that, **assuming solubility**, condition (H) is equivalent to
nilpotence of the nilpotent residual. Combining that published result
with the theorem here gives the same characterization for arbitrary
finite groups under the original hypothesis (H). The soluble case
is prior work, not an additional new candidate.

There is also closely related prior work of Bastos–Monetta,
*Coprime commutators in finite groups*, Comm. Algebra 47 (2019),
4137–4147, arXiv:1811.10025v1. Theorem A for k=2 treats all
commutators of coprime-order inputs, which may have composite orders,
and assumes equality of the output-order product. Its proof uses
the central-extension and local-subgroup strategy, and Proposition 18
already supplies an elementary abelian 2-subgroup with a nontrivial
odd-order action. These ingredients are prior. The theorem as stated
does not entail the weaker hypothesis of 19.56. Our use of the
hyperfocal theorem proves precisely the primary-input generation
needed here, and the explicit local constructions and lifts preserve
primary input orders. No novel classification or local-subgroup
existence theorem is claimed.

The argument here claims an affirmative candidate resolution of the
Notebook question. It does not claim priority over all subsequent
literature, and it has not been externally reviewed.
