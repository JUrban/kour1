# Problem 15.89: a positive dihedral case

Status: proved positive subclass. The separate `15.89-proof.md` now gives
a candidate negative answer to the full problem using C3*C2. No claim
of novelty for this subclass is made. The complete
question concerns every infinite connected locally finite vertex-transitive
simple graph. It acts on all complex-valued functions, with no boundedness
or summability restriction.

## Proposition

Every complex number is an eigenvalue of the adjacency operator of
every connected Cayley graph of the infinite dihedral group with a
finite inverse-closed generating set not containing the identity.

Write D_infinity=<r,s | s^2=1, srs=r^(-1)>. Such a generating set
consists of some rotations r^j (with nonzero exponents closed under
negation) and some reflections r^j s. Define Laurent polynomials

    p(z)=sum_(r^j in S) z^j,        q(z)=sum_(r^j s in S) z^j.

Thus p(z)=p(z^(-1)), its constant coefficient is zero, and all
coefficients of both p and q are zero or one. The function

    f(r^n)=u z^n,                  f(r^n s)=v z^n

has adjacency action on its coefficient vector (u,v) given by

    M(z) = [[p(z),q(z)],[q(z^(-1)),p(z)]].

This follows directly by multiplying vertices on the right by each
generator. Any z in C^* for which det(M(z)-lambda I)=0 therefore
gives a nonzero eigenfunction, by taking a nonzero vector in the
matrix kernel.

Let

    D(z)=(p(z)-lambda)^2-q(z)q(z^(-1)).

This is a symmetric Laurent polynomial. If it is nonconstant, writing
its highest exponent as d>0 shows that z^d D(z) is a polynomial of
degree 2d with nonzero constant coefficient. It has a nonzero complex
root. If D is identically zero, any nonzero z works. It remains only
to show that D cannot be a nonzero constant.

Multiplying q by a Laurent monomial does not change q(z)q(z^(-1)),
so assume q is a polynomial with constant and leading coefficients
one. There is at least one reflection because S generates the whole
dihedral group. Let m=degree(q).

If p=0, connectedness and infinitude force at least two reflections,
so m>0 and D is nonconstant. Suppose p!=0 and write R>0 for its
highest exponent. Then p^2 has highest exponent 2R, while q(z)q(z^(-1))
has highest exponent m. If m!=2R, their leading terms cannot cancel,
and the term -2 lambda p has smaller degree than 2R. Thus D is
nonconstant.

It remains to consider m=2R and assume D is constant. Put

    P(z)=z^R p(z).

The polynomial P has degree 2R, has coefficients in {0,1}, is
palindromic, has endpoints one, and has middle coefficient P_R=0.
Write q(z)=sum_i q_i z^i and P(z)=sum_i P_i z^i.

We prove inductively, for 0<=j<R, that

    q_j=q_(2R-j)=P_j=P_(2R-j).

This holds at j=0. For j>=1, compare the coefficients of z^(2R-j)
in q(z)q(z^(-1)) and p(z)^2. The terms involving lambda have no
such coefficient because 2R-j>R. Each coefficient is the sum over
i=0,...,j of the product of coefficients at i and 2R-j+i.
All interior products agree by the induction hypothesis. The two
endpoint terms give

    q_j+q_(2R-j)=2P_j.

The entries are zero or one, so both must equal P_j. This completes
the induction. Hence q=P+epsilon z^R, where epsilon is 0 or 1, and

    q(z)q(z^(-1))=(p(z)+epsilon)^2.

It follows that

    D(z)=-2(lambda+epsilon)p(z)+lambda^2-epsilon^2.

Because p is nonconstant, D is constant only when lambda=-epsilon.
For this value its constant value is zero. Therefore D is never a
nonzero constant, and every lambda has the required eigenfunction.

## Computation that suggested the coefficient argument

The exact search `scripts/search_15_89_dihedral.cpp` examines R=1,...,11.
It enumerates every possible polynomial q of degree 2R with endpoint
coefficients one and other coefficients zero or one. It compares their
autocorrelation coefficient vectors against every symmetric p with
highest exponent R and zero constant term. If all nonconstant terms
can cancel, then k=-2 lambda is an integer; the searched bound
-2R<=k<=2R+1 includes every possible coefficient difference.

Vector keys are exact strings of integer coefficients, so the result
does not depend on accepting a hash collision. For each candidate,
the remaining constant is computed exactly with denominator four.

Command:

    g++ -O3 -std=c++20 -mpopcnt scripts/search_15_89_dihedral.cpp -o /tmp/search_15_89_dihedral
    /tmp/search_15_89_dihedral 11

All 2,796,202 q-polynomials were checked. There were 4,094 constant
determinants and all were zero, with exactly 2^R such pairs at each R.
The proof above explains this pattern and supersedes any need to
extend this search. Output: `results/15.89-dihedral-search.log`.

## Literature and remaining scope

V. I. Trofimov, *On adjacency operators of locally finite graphs*,
Izvestiya: Mathematics 88:3 (2024), 542--589, Russian original
Izvestiya RAN: Ser. Mat. 88:3 (2024), 139--191;
https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01926v3 , DOI 10.4213/im9408e.
The Russian introduction, selected statements on propagators, and
examples in Sections 8.1--8.5 were read. The full proof of the general
theory was not audited. The English download endpoint returned 403.

The paper explicitly references Notebook 15.89. It distinguishes
the action on all vertex functions from the square-summable action,
and gives characteristic-zero counterexamples without vertex
transitivity. Its positive-characteristic vertex-transitive examples
do not apply to the complex-field question. The searched and read
sections did not locate this specific dihedral subclass; no priority
claim is inferred from that absence.

At the time of this subclass proof the main problem remained unresolved
here. The later C3*C2 construction in `15.89-proof.md` supplies an
explicit four-regular counterexample. A graph with leaves or a
periodic regular graph with several vertex orbits does not meet the
vertex-transitivity hypothesis. The dihedral reduction shows that a
two-by-two Cayley Laurent matrix of the above special form cannot
provide the desired counterexample.
