# A uniform large-prime answer to Problem 20.92(a)

Candidate affirmative resolution, 2026-09-11. Internal logical audit and exact
controls pass; outside review and broader novelty checking are pending.
Part (b) is prior work.

## 1. Statement

Let k>=2 and put

    E(k) = k^(2k-2),       D(k) = (k-1) E(k).

**Theorem.** Let A be an F_p-brace of dimension k. If p>k and the
multiplicative order of 2 modulo p is greater than D(k), then

    a dot b = - sum_{i=0}^{p-2} 2^(-i) ((2^i a)*b)

is a bilinear pre-Lie product on the original additive vector space A.
In particular this holds for every prime p>max(k,2^D(k)). For k=1
it holds for every odd prime, with zero product. Dimension zero is trivial.

Here a*b=a circle b-a-b, exactly as in the printed question on page 161.
No strong nilpotence or right nilpotence assumption is made. The numerical
bound is deliberately coarse; no optimal threshold is claimed.

## 2. A regular group of unipotent affine matrices

Write V=(A,+), and let lambda_a(b)=-a+a circle b. The brace law implies
that lambda_a is additive, hence F_p-linear, and

    lambda_(a circle b) = lambda_a lambda_b.

Consequently the matrices

    G_a = [ lambda_a  a ]
          [    0     1 ]

form a subgroup Gamma of the affine group: G_a G_b=G_(a circle b).
It has order p^k and acts regularly on V, since G_a sends 0 to a.

The image lambda(A) is a p-group. It preserves a complete flag on V and
acts trivially on the successive one-dimensional quotients. Here is an
elementary justification: for any nonzero F_p-module of a p-group, the
orbit equation says that the number of fixed vectors is divisible by p.
It includes zero, so there is a nonzero fixed vector. Apply this observation
successively to quotient modules. Choose a basis for the resulting flag.
All lambda_a are upper unitriangular in this basis, and Gamma is contained
in UT_(k+1)(F_p).

Let J be the associative algebra of strictly upper triangular (k+1)-by-(k+1)
matrices. It satisfies J^(k+1)=0. For p>k, the polynomial maps

    exp X = sum_{j=0}^k X^j/j!,
    log(1+N) = sum_{j=1}^k (-1)^(j+1) N^j/j

are mutually inverse between J and 1+J. Their induced multiplication on J
is the truncated BCH multiplication.

We use the classical Lazard subgroup correspondence: in nilpotency class
strictly less than p, logarithms take subgroups of a finite p-group to
Lie subrings of its Lazard Lie ring. In this matrix realization the Lie
ring is J with ordinary addition and [X,Y]=XY-YX. Thus

    g = log Gamma

is an F_p-linear Lie subalgebra of J. Its additive group is elementary
abelian (it is a subring of J), so being a Lie subring indeed implies
F_p-linearity. As exp is bijective, |g|=p^k and dim g=k.

The exact subgroup assertion is in Lazard (1954), pp.178-180,
especially the paragraph before Theorem 4.6 and that theorem. This is the
only general correspondence theorem imported into the proof.

Let pi:g->V take the last column above the bottom entry. This linear map
is injective: if pi(X)=0, exp(X) has translation zero, so regularity of
Gamma gives exp(X)=1 and X=0. Since both spaces have dimension k, pi is an
isomorphism. There is therefore a uniquely determined linear map

    x |-> L_x in End(V)

such that every element of g has the form

    X_x = [ L_x  x ] .
          [  0   0 ]

All L_x are strictly upper triangular k-by-k matrices.

## 3. The pre-Lie algebra and the exponential translation map

Taking the matrix commutator of X_x and X_y, and using Lie closure and
the last column to identify the resulting element, gives

    [L_x,L_y] = L_(L_x y-L_y x).                         (1)

Define x triangleright y=L_x y. This is bilinear. Applying (1) to z and
rearranging gives

    (x triangleright y) triangleright z
      - x triangleright (y triangleright z)
    = (y triangleright x) triangleright z
      - y triangleright (x triangleright z),

the required pre-Lie identity. It is also left nilpotent, since any k
successive left multiplications vanish.

The exponential has the block form

    exp(X_x) = [ exp(L_x)  W(x) ],
               [    0       1  ]

where

    W(x) = sum_{j=0}^{k-1} L_x^j x/(j+1)!.              (2)

This is an ordinary polynomial map of total degree at most k, with
constant term zero and linear homogeneous part x. It is a bijection on
V: the maps x->X_x, exp:g->Gamma and G_a->a are all bijections.
Writing Omega=W^(-1) as a function for now, we have

    lambda_a = exp(L_(Omega(a))).                       (3)

We next produce a polynomial representing Omega whose degree depends
only on k. Finite-set interpolation would give a p-dependent bound and
would not suffice here.

## 4. A polynomial inverse with a dimension-only degree bound

For independent vector variables x,y, define

    C(x,y) = pi log(exp(X_x) exp(X_y)).

This is a polynomial of total degree at most k. Indeed every monomial
in the matrix expansion is a product of copies of X_x and X_y, each
linear in the variables. All products of more than k such strictly
upper triangular matrices vanish. All denominators appearing in the
truncated exponential and logarithm are invertible when p>k.

The BCH identity and Lie closure imply the **formal polynomial identity**

    X_(C(x,y)) = log(exp(X_x) exp(X_y)).                 (4)

This assertion is valid over the polynomial coefficient ring, rather
than merely on its F_p-points: the truncated BCH expression is a Lie
polynomial with coefficients whose denominators have no prime factor
greater than k, and the linear space g is closed under each bracket.
Equivalently one extends g and its matrix embedding by scalars. Matrix
exponentiation of (4), followed by taking the translation column, gives

    W(C(x,y)) = W(x)+exp(L_x) W(y).                     (5)

Set V_i=span(e_1,...,e_(k+1-i)) for 1<=i<=k+1, so V_1=V and
V_(k+1)=0. Every L_x sends V_i into V_(i+1). For r in V_i, formula (2)
gives

    W(r) in V_i,            W(r)-r in V_(i+1),
    (exp(L_x)-I)W(r) in V_(i+1).                        (6)

These inclusions hold over every commutative F_p-algebra, with V_i
replaced by its scalar extension. They do not assert, or require,
L_(V_i)(V_j) contained in V_(i+j).

Now let a be a vector of k independent indeterminates. Define polynomials
recursively by

    x_0(a)=0;
    r_(i-1)(a)=a-W(x_(i-1)(a));
    x_i(a)=C(x_(i-1)(a),r_(i-1)(a)),       1<=i<=k.

Induction gives r_(i-1) in V_i. The base case is r_0=a in V_1.
Using (5) for the induction step gives

    r_i = r_(i-1)-exp(L_(x_(i-1)))W(r_(i-1)),

which belongs to V_(i+1) by (6). Hence r_k=0, and

    W(x_k(a))=a                                          (7)

as a polynomial identity. On the finite set V, uniqueness of W's inverse
shows that Omega(a)=x_k(a).

For the degree estimate, C(0,a)=a, so x_1=a has degree one.
If deg x_i<=e_i with e_i>=1, then

    deg r_i <= k e_i,
    deg x_(i+1) <= k max(e_i,k e_i) = k^2 e_i.

It follows that deg Omega<=k^(2k-2)=E(k). Each x_i has constant
term zero. Since W has linear part the identity, (7) also implies that
Omega has linear homogeneous part a.

Combining this with (3), the matrix polynomial

    P(a)=exp(L_(Omega(a)))-I

has constant term zero, total degree at most (k-1)E(k)=D(k), and linear
homogeneous part L_a. Its values are precisely lambda_a-I.

The residual-correction idea is closely related to Trappeniers' proof of
regularity under the Lazard correspondence (v1 Proposition 8.10; v3
Proposition 5.5). The degree estimate and its application below are the
additional steps used here.

## 5. The printed average recovers exactly L_a

Write the chosen formal matrix polynomial as

    P(a)=sum_{j=1}^{D(k)} P_j(a),

where P_j is homogeneous of total degree j; zero components are allowed.
Let d=ord_p(2)>D(k). For j=1,

    sum_{i=0}^{p-2} 2^(i(j-1)) = p-1 = -1 in F_p.

For 2<=j<=D(k), we have 2^(j-1) != 1, while
2^((p-1)(j-1))=1. The geometric sum is therefore zero. Consequently

    -sum_{i=0}^{p-2} 2^(-i) P(2^i a) = P_1(a)=L_a.

Applying this matrix equality to b proves that the operation in the
question is exactly a triangleright b from Section 3. In particular it
uses the original addition, rather than addition transported by Omega.
There is no primitive-root assumption on 2.

Finally, if p>2^D(k) and d<=D(k), then p divides the positive integer
2^d-1<2^D(k)<p, a contradiction. This proves the stated uniform bound.
For k=1, lambda(A) is a p-subgroup of F_p^*, hence trivial, so every
summand is zero. This completes the proof.

## 6. Prior work and claimed scope

Lazard's classical subgroup theorem is an explicit dependency. The
affine interpretation, correspondence, and group-of-flows formula are
prior machinery, developed for braces in particular by Trappeniers.
The weighted coefficient-extraction method is also prior: see
Trappeniers v3 Lemma 6.1 and Smoktunowicz's work cited there.

Trappeniers v3 Proposition 6.2 assumes the stronger condition
A_i*A_j contained in A_(i+j); the paragraph following it identifies
the brace case with strong nilpotence. The present argument supplies
a dimension-only polynomial bound using only simultaneous strict
triangularity of the L_x. It consequently addresses the additional
non-strongly-nilpotent case expressly left open in the supplied 2025
comment on 20.92(a). This is the candidate new consequence; the
correspondence in part (b) itself is not claimed as new.
