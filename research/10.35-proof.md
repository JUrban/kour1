# A candidate negative answer to Problem 10.35

Date: 2026-09-10. Status: complete elementary candidate proof, awaiting
independent review and further novelty checking.

## The precise question

Problem 10.35 (G. A. Noskov), on printed and physical PDF page 40 of
the supplied September 2026 Kourovka Notebook, asks whether every
finitely generated torsion-free subgroup of GL_n(C) is residually in
the class of torsion-free subgroups of GL_n(Q). The same n occurs
on both sides. The page was checked visually; this entry has no star
or additional qualification.

Here is a counterexample for n=2. In fact it is not residually in
GL_2(R), even if the target subgroups are allowed to have torsion.

## An explicit two-generator torsion-free group

For z in Z[i], put

    U(z) = [ 1  z ],       T = [ 2i  0 ].
           [ 0  1 ]           [  0  2 ]

Let a=U(1) and G=<a,T> <= GL_2(C). Since

    T U(z) T^{-1} = U(iz),

the group contains b=T a T^{-1}=U(i). It consists exactly of the
matrices U(z)T^k with z in Z[i] and k in Z. Multiplication is

    (z,k)(w,l) = (z+i^k w,k+l).

These expressions are unique: the lower diagonal entry 2^k determines
k, and then the upper right entry determines z. Thus

    G = Z^2 semidirect_J Z,       J = [ 0 -1 ].
                                      [ 1  0 ]

In particular G is generated by two elements. It is torsion-free:
an element U(z)T^k of finite order must have 2^k a root of unity,
which forces k=0; and a nonidentity U(z) has infinite order in
characteristic zero. Alternatively, this follows directly from the
torsion-free normal subgroup Z^2 and torsion-free quotient Z.

The subgroup A={U(z):z in Z[i]} is abelian and normal, and

    [a,TaT^{-1}]=1,       T^2 a T^{-2}=a^{-1}.                 (1)

Notice that T itself has infinite order: T^4=16I. Replacing it by
diag(i,1) would introduce torsion and would not answer the question.

## A two-dimensional matrix obstruction

**Lemma.** Let F be a field of characteristic zero in which -1 is
not a square. If X,Y in GL_2(F) satisfy

    [X,YXY^{-1}]=I,       Y^2 X Y^{-2}=X^{-1},                (2)

then X^2=I.

**Proof.** Suppose X^2 is not I. If X were scalar, the second relation
would immediately give X=X^{-1}, a contradiction. Thus X is nonscalar.

Conjugacy of X and X^{-1} gives det(X)^2=1. If det(X)=-1, then

    tr(X)=tr(X^{-1})=tr(X)/det(X)=-tr(X),

so tr(X)=0. The characteristic polynomial is then t^2-1, and the
Cayley--Hamilton identity gives X^2=I, again a contradiction. Hence
det(X)=1, and Cayley--Hamilton gives

    X^{-1}=tr(X) I-X.                                       (3)

The centralizer algebra of a nonscalar two by two matrix X is the
two-dimensional algebra E=F[I,X]. For completeness, choose a vector
v for which v,Xv are linearly independent. Such a vector exists,
since otherwise every vector would be an eigenvector and X would
be scalar. A matrix commuting with X is determined by its value
on v, so its centralizer has dimension at most two; I and X give
two linearly independent members.

Set X_1=YXY^{-1}. The first relation in (2) puts X_1 in E. It is
nonscalar, so F[I,X_1]=E. Consequently conjugation by Y maps E
onto E, fixing its scalar subspace FI pointwise. On the
one-dimensional quotient E/FI it therefore acts as multiplication
by some q in F^*.

The class of X in E/FI is nonzero. The second relation in (2) and
(3) say that the square of this induced action sends that class to
its negative. Thus q^2=-1, contrary to the hypothesis on F. This
proves the lemma. QED.

## Failure of the required residual property

Take any homomorphism phi:G -> GL_2(Q). Apply the lemma with
X=phi(a) and Y=phi(T), using (1). It follows that

    phi(a^2)=I.

But a^2=U(2) is not I. Therefore this single nonidentity element
lies in the kernel of every homomorphism from G into GL_2(Q).
This already rules out residuality with respect to all subgroups
of GL_2(Q), and in particular its torsion-free subgroups.

More precisely, if the image is torsion-free then phi(a)^2=I
implies phi(a)=I. It also kills b=TaT^{-1}, so it kills the whole
normal subgroup A. Every such image is therefore cyclic, as
G/A is infinite cyclic. This proves the requested negative answer.

Exactly the same proof works with Q replaced by R, or by any
characteristic-zero field not containing a square root of -1.

## Controls and scope

The example is metabelian, polycyclic, and virtually free abelian:
A times <T^4> is a subgroup of index four isomorphic to Z^3.
It even has the finite presentation

    <a,b,T | [a,b]=1, TaT^{-1}=b, TbT^{-1}=a^{-1}>.

The fixed matrix degree is essential to this argument. For example
G does embed in GL_3(Q). For v=(x,y)^t corresponding to z=x+iy,
put

    V(v) = [ I_2  v ],       S = diag(2J,2).
           [  0   1 ]

Then S V(v) S^{-1}=V(Jv), and (z,k) maps to V(v)S^k. This
representation is faithful: its bottom right entry determines k,
and then the last column determines v. Thus the construction does
not obstruct a version of the question allowing the rational degree
to increase.

The stronger conclusion that every rational representation kills
a itself would be false without the torsion-free image hypothesis.
Indeed a -> diag(-1,1), b -> diag(1,-1), and
T -> [[0,1],[1,0]] obey the presentation and give an image of
order eight. The element a^2 is the witness valid for arbitrary
images. Over Q(i), our original X=a,Y=T satisfy (2) with X of
infinite order; here the induced scalar in the lemma is i, exactly
where its field hypothesis fails.
