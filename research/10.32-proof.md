## Result

Let r,s be nonzero integers, with at least one odd, and put
m=rad(|rs|), the product of the distinct primes dividing |rs|.
Then x^r y^s is universal on S_n whenever

    n >= max{1126,16 log m}.                                      (1)

All logarithms are natural. More generally, for every real c>8 there
is N_0(c) such that universality holds for

    n >= max{N_0(c),c log m}.                                     (2)

Either exponent equal to +1 or -1 gives universality in every degree.
If both exponents are odd, every degree works as well. The constants
in (1) are sufficient, not claimed optimal.

## Imported results

**Cycle-product existence.** Suppose H,K are conjugacy classes in S_d,
with respectively h,k cycles, counting fixed points. If

    h+k+1 <= L <= d,    L == h+k-1 (mod 2),

then some alpha in H and beta in K have product an L-cycle.
This is the positive-genus, three-partition case of Boccara1982,
as reproved by Song--Xu--Ye, arXiv2210.09700v2 (6 February2024),
Propositions1.4 and2.1. Indeed take their m_1=L-1 and
g=(L-h-k+1)/2>=1. Their three permutations satisfy
tau_1 tau_2 sigma=1; the inverse of sigma is the required cycle.
Their transitivity conclusion is stronger than needed here.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.09700v2

**Even-target covering.** If u,v>=4 and
floor(3n/4)+1 <= u+v <= n, then every element of A_n is a product
of two permutations, each having one u-cycle and one disjoint v-cycle,
with all other points fixed. This is Lemma5 of
Brenner--Evans--Silberger, Proc.AMS96(1986),23--28.
https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~revans/Universality.pdf

**Prime estimates.** Write theta(x)=sum_{p<=x} log p. Rosser--Schoenfeld,
Illinois J.Math.6(1962),64--94, Theorem4, inequalities(3.14)--(3.15),
gives theta(x)>x(1-1/(2log x)) for x>=563 and
theta(x)<x(1+1/(2log x)) for x>1. Theorem9,(3.32), gives
theta(x)<1.01624x for x>0. In particular theta(x)/x tends to1.
https://doi.org/10.1215/ijm/1255631807

Only these statements are imported. The application below is proved
in full; it does not require a genus-zero Hurwitz theorem.

## Lemma: odd permutations

Let q>=3 be odd, and suppose

    3n/8+1 < q <= n/2.                                          (3)

Every odd permutation in S_n is a product of a 2q-cycle and a
permutation consisting of two disjoint q-cycles.

Proof. Let g be odd. If g is a transposition, choose two disjoint
q-cycles whose respective supports contain its two letters, and let
their product be B. Multiplication by g merges the two cycles of
B^{-1}, so A=gB^{-1} is a 2q-cycle and g=AB.

Now assume g is not a transposition. Let s_0 be its support size
and t its number of nontrivial cycles. Its transposition length

    e=s_0-t

is a positive odd integer; since g is not a transposition, e>=3.
Also t<=s_0/2, since every nontrivial cycle has length at least2.

Choose an invariant subset of size d=max{s_0,2q} containing its
support; this is possible since d<=n. On this subset g has
h=t+d-s_0 cycles. The class K of two disjoint q-cycles has
k=d-2q+2 cycles. We verify the cycle-product criterion with L=2q.

If s_0<=2q, then d=2q and

    h+k+1 = (2q-e)+2+1 <= 2q.

If s_0>2q, then d=s_0 and

    h+k+1 = s_0+t-2q+3 <= 3n/2-2q+3 < 2q,

where the last inequality follows from4q>3n/2+4 in (3).
In both cases L<=d. Finally

    h+k-1 = 2d-e-2q+1

is even, so the parity condition holds. The imported theorem gives
alpha conjugate to g and beta in K such that alpha beta=A is a
2q-cycle. Hence alpha=A beta^{-1}. Simultaneous conjugation and
extension by fixed points give the desired factorization of g in S_n.
This proves the lemma. QED

The two support cases are essential: applying the theorem in the
full degree n without first removing unnecessary fixed points
would impose a false restriction on small-support targets.

## Lemma: a missing prime gives all targets

Suppose q>=5 is prime, satisfies(3), and q does not divide rs.
Assume first that r is odd. For an odd target, the preceding lemma
gives g=AB with orders2q andq. For an even target, apply the
even-target covering theorem with u=v=q. Its inequalities hold
because2q>3n/4+2 and2q<=n, and both resulting factors have orderq.
Consequently every target has a factorization g=AB with

    gcd(r,ord(A))=gcd(s,ord(B))=1.

Choose integers R,S satisfying rR==1 modulo ord(A) and
sS==1 modulo ord(B). Then g=(A^R)^r(B^S)^s, as required.
This also covers negative exponents.

If s is the odd exponent, apply the preceding argument to the pair
(s,r), obtaining g=U^s V^r. The identity

    U^s V^r = V^r (V^{-r} U V^r)^s

gives the required ordering. QED

## Explicit prime-product bound

Put x=n/2 and y=3n/8+1. If n>=1126, then x>=563 and log x>6.
For example, e<11/4, as follows from its convergent factorial series,
and (11/4)^6<563. Thus Theorem4 gives

    theta(x) > 11n/24.

Theorem9 and the rational inequality1.01624<49/48 give

    theta(y) < (49/48)(3n/8+1) = 49n/128+49/48.

Subtracting,

    theta(x)-theta(y) > 29n/384-49/48 > n/16,                   (4)

since the last inequality is equivalent to5n>392.

Every prime in (y,x] is at least5 and satisfies(3). If all these
primes divided m, then

    log m >= theta(x)-theta(y) > n/16,

contrary to n>=16log m. Therefore some prime in the interval
does not divide m, and the missing-prime lemma proves(1).
The strict inequality also handles equality in n=16log m.

The elementary exponential estimate used above is fully rational:
sum_{j=0}^3 1/j!=8/3 and
sum_{j=4}^infinity 1/j! <= (1/24)sum_{a=0}^infinity(1/5)^a=5/96.
Hence e<=87/32<11/4, and11^6=1771561<563*4^6=2306048.

## Asymptotic constants

The two bounds in Rosser--Schoenfeld Theorem4 imply theta(z)=z+o(z).
Consequently

    theta(n/2)-theta(3n/8+1) = n/8+o(n).

For each c>8, choose N_0(c)>=1126 so that the left side exceeds n/c
for every n>=N_0(c). If n>=c log m, all primes in this interval
cannot divide m. The same missing-prime lemma proves(2).
No uniform assertion is made with c=8.

## Both odd exponents

For completeness, each cycle has an involutory reversing permutation:
identify its support with Z/aZ and use j -> -j. The disjoint product
t of these reversals is an involution satisfying tgt=g^{-1}.
Then gt is also an involution and g=(gt)t. If r and s are both odd,
g=(gt)^r t^s. Either exponent +/-1 is immediate independently.

## Scope

This supplies the requested logarithmic form, with weaker constants
than the alternating-group bound quoted in the Notebook. It does not
assert that the roots are each single cycles, as in Droste's original
construction: a product of two disjoint q-cycles is allowed, exactly
as the word problem permits. The infinite range is proved by the
imported theorems and the deductions above, not by finite computation.
Priority of the resulting bound is unconfirmed.
