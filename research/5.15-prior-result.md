# Problem 5.15: affirmative consequence of a published theorem

Status: prior result, excluded from the new-candidate count.
Checked 11 September 2026.
The exact supplied statement on printed page 15 was visually checked.

The supplied Notebook asks for a finitely presented residually finite group
whose word problem is recursive but has no primitive recursive solution.
Kharlampovich, Myasnikov and Sapir's *Algorithmically complex residually
finite groups*, Bulletin of Mathematical Sciences (2017),
[DOI 10.1007/s13373-017-0103-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13373-017-0103-z),
implies an affirmative answer. A primary reading copy is available from
[the German National Library](https://d-nb.info/1129545873/34).

Their Theorem 4.21 supplies, for every recursive set X, a finitely presented
residually finite solvable group of derived length three whose word problem
is as hard as membership in X and polynomially reduces to it. Definition
2.2(a) gives the precise algorithmic meaning of the first assertion.
Theorem 4.18 supplies the underlying polynomial reductions between the
group word problem and machine configuration equivalence. The statements
and proofs of 4.18 and 4.21 were read; the complete earlier machine and
semigroup construction has not been independently audited.

Here is the deduction of the particular property in 5.15. Effectively
enumerate the unary primitive recursive functions as f_0,f_1,..., allowing
repetitions, using their finite construction expressions. The total
evaluator for these expressions is recursive. Set

    chi_X(i) = 1 - (f_i(i) mod 2).

Then X is recursive. Its characteristic function cannot be primitive
recursive: if it were f_j, its value at j would differ from f_j(j).

A primitive recursive decision function admits an algorithm with a
primitive recursive time bound. This follows by induction over its
construction: composition combines such bounds, and primitive recursion
iterates the appropriate bounds a specified finite number of times.
Conversely, a Turing machine with a primitive recursive time bound can be
simulated by bounded iteration, so its decision function is primitive
recursive. These assertions apply to the usual finite-string encodings;
changing between unary natural numbers and binary encodings preserves
primitive recursiveness.

Apply Theorem 4.21 to this X. If its group's word problem had a primitive
recursive solution, the time comparison in Definition 2.2(a) would give a
decision algorithm for X with a primitive recursive time bound: constant
rescaling of the input and multiplication or addition by fixed linear
functions preserve this class of bounds. The preceding paragraph gives a
contradiction. The group's word problem is recursive by the other
reduction (also by finite presentation and residual finiteness).

This deduction uses the theorem's word-problem assertion. A large Dehn
function alone would not justify the conclusion.
