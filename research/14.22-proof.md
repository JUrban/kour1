# Problem 14.22: a coefficient-support obstruction

Status: complete candidate negative answer to the printed statement,
including a nonabelian coefficient group. Internal audit and exact
controls pass; novelty and independent outside review remain pending.
The coefficient groups are not finitely generated. See
`14.22-review.md` and `results/14.22-verification.json`.

## Statement and conventions

The supplied Notebook, PDF page 75, asks whether every irreducible system
over a torsion-free linear group G is equivalent over G to a finite system
T for which Rad_G(T) equals the least normal isolated subgroup sqrt(T) of
G * F(X) containing T. The page has been inspected visually. No finite
generation or noncommutativity hypothesis on G appears in the statement.

Here Rad_G(T) is the intersection of the kernels of evaluations at all
solutions in G. A normal subgroup N of a group P is isolated precisely
when P/N is torsion-free. Irreducible refers to the solution set with its
group-equation Zariski topology. We also verify discrimination directly,
so the example does not depend on a decomposition criterion imported
from a theorem with additional hypotheses.

## The example

Write the additive group of rational numbers multiplicatively as

    G = {a_q : q in Q},     a_q a_r = a_(q+r).

This group is torsion-free and embeds into SL_2(Q) by

    a_q -> [[1,q],[0,1]].

Take one variable x and the single equation S = {[x,a_1]}. Its solution
set in G is all of G. This avoids any convention about an empty system.

For every finite T in G * <x> with V_G(T) = G, we will prove

    sqrt(T) is a proper subgroup of Rad_G(T).

## Irreducibility

For a word w in G * <x>, let m(w) be its total exponent sum in x and let
b(w) be the sum of its rational coefficients. At x = a_t its value is

    w(a_t) = a_(m(w)t + b(w)).

Thus an equation in one variable has either no solutions, one solution,
or all of G as solutions. The same holds for an arbitrary system.
The topology whose closed sets are arbitrary intersections of finite
unions of algebraic sets is therefore the cofinite topology on Q.
Since Q is infinite, G is irreducible. It also cannot be covered by
finitely many proper algebraic subsets, each of which has at most one
point.

Equivalently, the coordinate G-group of this line is G x Z: the natural
map G * <x> -> G x Z has kernel exactly the words with m(w)=b(w)=0.
For any finite list of nonzero pairs (b_i,m_i), there is a rational t
such that b_i + m_i t is nonzero for every i. Evaluation at t is a
G-homomorphism G x Z -> G separating the entire list simultaneously.
Thus the coordinate group is G-discriminated by G, directly.

## No finite equivalent system has the requested radical

Let T be any finite system with V_G(T)=G. Every word w in T is an
identity on G, so evaluating at t=0 and t=1 gives b(w)=m(w)=0.

Only finitely many rational coefficients occur in T. Choose a positive
integer d for which all of them lie in the cyclic subgroup

    C = <a_(1/d)>.

If T has no nonidentity coefficients, take d=1. Put

    A = C x <x>,                 K = G *_C A.

There is a natural surjective G-homomorphism

    pi: G * <x> -> K.

Every word of T lies in C * <x> and has zero exponent sum in each
of its two generators a_(1/d) and x. Consequently it vanishes in A,
and hence in K. Thus T is contained in N=ker(pi).

Both G and A are torsion-free. The normal form theorem for amalgamated
free products implies that their amalgam K is torsion-free: an element
is conjugate either to a factor element or to a cyclically reduced word
of syllable length at least two. In the latter case its nonzero powers
are reduced words of positive length. In the former case finite order
is impossible because the factors embed and are torsion-free.

It follows that N is a normal isolated subgroup containing T, and so

    sqrt(T) <= N.

On the other hand, put r=a_(1/(2d)). Since G is abelian,

    z = [x,r] = x^(-1) r^(-1) x r

vanishes at every solution of T in G. Therefore z belongs to Rad_G(T).
But x and x^(-1) lie in A outside C, and r and r^(-1) lie in G outside
C. Their alternating word is reduced of length four in G *_C A.
The normal form theorem gives pi(z) != 1. Hence

    z is not in N, and in particular z is not in sqrt(T).

Since the radical of a system over a torsion-free group is itself a
normal isolated subgroup containing the system, sqrt(T) <= Rad_G(T).
The exhibited element proves strict inclusion. This holds for every
finite equivalent T, and gives the asserted negative answer.

## Important scope distinctions

1. The example uses a torsion-free linear group of rational matrices;
   linearity over an exotic field is unnecessary.
2. G is not finitely generated. This is permitted by the printed
   statement. The argument does not resolve a version restricted to
   finitely generated coefficient groups.
3. The separating group K need only be torsion-free. The definition of
   sqrt(T) does not require its quotient to be linear, abelian, or to
   have unique roots.
4. T is allowed to use any finite collection of coefficients of G,
   including coefficients absent from S. The common denominator d is
   chosen after T, so these extra coefficients do not avoid the argument.
5. The variable set is the fixed one in the question. Equivalence here
   is equality of solution sets in G, as in the cited group-algebraic
   geometry definition; it is not equivalence after existentially
   quantifying auxiliary variables.
6. Commuting with the square of r does not force commuting with r in
   an arbitrary torsion-free group. In this example x commutes with
   r^2=a_(1/d) but does not commute with r in K. Isolatedness concerns
   torsion in a quotient, not uniqueness of roots in that quotient.

## A nonabelian linear version

The same obstruction holds for G=Q * <b>, so it is not an artifact of
using an abelian coefficient group. We give all necessary separation
arguments instead of invoking a theorem about centralizer extensions.

For an element q of a field, write U(q)=[[1,q],[0,1]], and let
L(t)=[[1,0],[t,1]]. With t transcendental over Q, the assignments

    a_q -> U(q),                 b -> L(t)

embed Q * <b> in SL_2(Q(t)). To see faithfulness, cyclically reduce
a nontrivial free-product word. A word conjugate into a factor has
nonidentity image. Otherwise its cyclically reduced form has the shape

    U(q_1)L(n_1 t) ... U(q_k)L(n_k t),

where all q_i and n_i are nonzero. Its trace is a polynomial in t of
degree k with nonzero leading coefficient product_i(q_i n_i). It
therefore is not the identity matrix. The coefficient formula follows
by taking the degree-one term from each factor U(q_i)L(n_i t): that
term, divided by n_i t, is [[q_i,0],[1,0]], and the trace of their
product is product_i(q_i).

G is torsion-free by free-product normal form. The centralizer in G of
a_1 is exactly Q. Indeed, a free factor is malnormal: if g is outside
Q, its reduced form shows g^(-1) a_1 g is outside Q. Thus the equation

    S = {[x,a_1]}

has solution set Q inside this nonabelian G.

Put H=(Q x <x>) * <b>. Its evident G-group structure is well defined.
We claim that the maps H -> G given by x -> a_q for q in Q
G-discriminate H. Introduce a second independent indeterminate z and
represent H by

    (a_q,x^n) -> U(q+n z),       b -> L(t).

This representation in SL_2(Q(z,t)) is faithful by the same trace
argument: every nonzero pair (q,n) gives a nonzero polynomial q+n z.
For a nontrivial h in H, choose a nonzero entry of its matrix minus I.
It is a polynomial in z and t with rational coefficients. At least
one coefficient when expanded in t is a nonzero polynomial in z.
Consequently h remains nonidentity after z=q for all but finitely
many rational q. A finite list of nontrivial elements has only finitely
many bad specializations altogether. This proves discrimination.

Let eta:G * <x> -> H be the natural map. Its kernel is precisely
Rad_G(S). Inclusion in the radical is immediate because x=a_q
commutes with Q. The reverse inclusion follows from discrimination:
if eta(w) != 1, some rational specialization x=a_q makes w nontrivial.

The set V_G(S)=Q is irreducible. For a direct proof, any proper
algebraic subset of it is defined by a system containing some word
w which does not vanish identically on Q. Its image eta(w) is nontrivial
and, by the polynomial argument, w vanishes on at most finitely many
points of Q. Thus every proper relative algebraic subset is finite;
the same holds for proper relatively closed subsets in the generated
Zariski topology. Q is infinite, so it is irreducible. Alternatively,
discrimination itself rules out a finite cover by proper algebraic
subsets, by choosing one nonidentically vanishing equation from each.

Now take any finite T equivalent to S over G. Decompose its finitely
many coefficient words into rational and b syllables. Choose d>0 so
every rational syllable lies in C=<a_(1/d)>. Then

    T is contained in (C * <b>) * <x>.

Its image in H is trivial, because T is contained in Rad_G(S).
The natural homomorphism

    H_0=(C x <x>) * <b> -> (Q x <x>) * <b>=H

is injective by free-product normal form. Therefore each word of T
already vanishes in H_0.

Define the torsion-free G-group

    K = (Q *_C (C x <x>)) * <b>.

The factor embeddings and free-product normal form give an embedding
G=Q * <b> -> K. The group H_0 also embeds in K, so all equations T
vanish under the natural map pi:G * <x> -> K. As before, ker(pi) is
a normal isolated subgroup containing T. But [x,a_(1/(2d))] has a
reduced length-four word in Q *_C (C x <x>), so is nontrivial in K.
It belongs to Rad_G(T) since all solutions are in Q. Thus

    sqrt(T) < Rad_G(T)

for every finite equivalent T, also with this nonabelian linear G.

The group Q * Z is also a CSA group: nontrivial elements conjugate
into its torsion-free abelian factors have those factors as malnormal
centralizers, and other nontrivial elements have cyclic malnormal
centralizers, as follows from the free-product tree. This additional
observation is not needed for the proof of irreducibility, which was
given directly. The proof does not assert a finitely generated example.

## References and audit

The definitions of isolated normal subgroup, radical, equivalence, and
the Nullstellensatz are in the introductory definitions of
Kharlampovich--Myasnikov, *Irreducible affine varieties over a free
group. I: Irreducibility of quadratic equations and Nullstellensatz*,
J. Algebra 200 (1998), 472--516 (Notebook cites both parts through 570),
author PDF:
https://web.stevens.edu/algebraic/alexeim/Publications/All_files_new/pp1_34.pdf .
Its preliminary geometry restricts G to nonabelian CSA groups.
Baumslag--Myasnikov--Remeslennikov, *Algebraic geometry over groups I:
Algebraic sets and ideal theory*, J. Algebra 219 (1999), 16--79,
defines the Zariski topology for arbitrary G-groups by taking algebraic
sets as a subbasis of closed sets (Sections 1.6 and 3.1), and defines
irreducibility topologically (Section 1.8). These definitions explicitly
allow the first example above. Author PDF:
https://web.stevens.edu/algebraic/alexeim/Publications/All_files_new/FINAL_AL_51_38.pdf .
The nonabelian version additionally avoids dependence on extending the
coefficient-group scope of Kharlampovich--Myasnikov's preliminary
discussion.

The present argument uses the standard amalgamated-free-product normal
form theorem. Its two needed consequences are explicitly explained
above. Broader literature checking remains necessary before assigning
any novelty status.
