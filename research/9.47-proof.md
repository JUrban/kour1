# Candidate negative answer to Problem 9.47

2026-09-11. Complete deduction from Protasov's neighborhood lemma
and the Dow--Watson non-Skula compact space. Both the needed
neighborhood lemma and the counterexample's obstruction are
proved explicitly below, following their primary sources. These
ingredients are prior; novelty of their application to9.47 and
outside review remain pending.

## 1. Exact statement and topology

There is a compact Hausdorff scattered space X of Cantor--Bendixson
height four that is not homeomorphic to any subspace of the space
of closed noncompact subgroups of any locally compact Hausdorff
group, when that subgroup space has the E-topology.

More precisely, a compact Hausdorff space embeds in such a subgroup
space if and only if it has a clopen selector, as defined below.
For the positive direction a discrete abelian group always suffices.

For a locally compact group G write L(G) for its closed subgroups.
The E-topology is the topology with subbasic open sets

    D_1(O) = {H in L(G): H is contained in O},
    D_2(O) = {H in L(G): H intersects O},

where O ranges over all open subsets of G. This is the full
Vietoris topology. Protasov's original paper, cited below, gives
exactly this definition in its opening paragraph. It is not the
Chabauty topology, whose upper subbasis permits only avoidance of
compact sets. Notebook page35 and the original definition were
visually checked.

The space L(G) is Hausdorff. If H and K are distinct, take, for
example, g in H minus K and an open neighborhood V of g whose
closure misses K. Then D_2(V) and D_1(G minus closure(V)) are
disjoint neighborhoods of H and K. Such V exists because a
Hausdorff topological group is regular.

For any subset A of G we still use D_1(A) to denote containment,
without asserting that D_1(A) is open. In particular, for a closed
subgroup H it is always closed in L(G), because its complement is
D_2(G minus H).

A clopen selector on a space X is an assignment x -> U_x with:

1. U_x is clopen and contains x;
2. if x and y are distinct, they do not both satisfy x in U_y and
   y in U_x;
3. y in U_x implies U_y is contained in U_x.

For compact Hausdorff spaces this is equivalent to being a Skula
space (Dow--Watson, Theorem1). The negative argument uses these
three explicit conditions directly, so does not depend on any
convention for the word Skula.

## 2. The subgroup-space neighborhood lemma

Protasov, Lemma2: if F is a subspace of L(G), H is a noncompact
member of F, and H has a countably compact neighborhood in F, then
D_1(H) intersect F is a neighborhood of H in F.

Reference: I. V. Protasov, *Compact subspaces in the space of
subgroups of a topological group*, Ukrainian Mathematical Journal
38, 512--516, translation of the 1986 Russian paper on pp.600--605.
The translated issue carries a1987 copyright date. Lemma2 is on
page512, its precise hypotheses were visually checked, and the
entire paper through its references was read:
https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF01060943.pdf .

Its printed proof uses Lemma2 of Yu. A. Komarov and I. V. Protasov,
*Compactness in the lattice of subgroups of a topological group*,
Ukrainian Mathematical Journal 33 (1981), 145--149, translated from
pp.184--189:
https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF01086071.pdf .
The setup and entire Section1, including the full proof of Lemma2,
were read. For completeness we now give the compact-family case
in full, following that argument. No group classification or
additional subgroup-space theorem is needed in this proof.

**Lemma.** Let F be a compact subspace of L(G), and let H in F
be noncompact. Then D_1(H) intersect F is a neighborhood of H
in F. The other members of F need not be noncompact.

**Proof.** Suppose the conclusion fails. Put

    A = {K in F: K is not contained in H}.

Then H is in the closure of A in F. For each open neighborhood W
of H in F put

    E_W = union over K in A intersect W of (K minus H).

First E_W cannot be relatively compact in G. Otherwise its closure
C would be compact. Choose h in H outside the compact set
C union C^(-1)C; this is possible because H is closed and
noncompact. Choose an open identity neighborhood V such that Vh
misses both C and C^(-1)C. In particular C Vh misses C.

Since H belongs to D_2(Vh) and is in the closure of A, there is
some K in A intersect W intersect D_2(Vh). Choose x in K intersect
Vh. If x were outside H it would lie in E_W, and thus in C,
contrary to the choice of Vh. Hence x lies in H. Choose z in
K minus H. Then z lies in C and zx lies in K minus H, because
H is a subgroup and x is in H. Thus zx lies in E_W, a subset
of C, and also lies in C Vh, which misses C. This contradiction
proves the assertion about E_W.

Fix a compact identity neighborhood U. Inductively choose K_i
in A, x_i in K_i minus H, and a compact neighborhood V_i of x_i
disjoint from H. At step i require

    K_i in the intersection over j<i of D_1(G minus V_j),
    x_i U intersect x_j U = empty for every j<i.

The first requirement is an open neighborhood W_i of H, considered
relative to F. The set E_(W_i) is not relatively compact, so it
contains an element x_i outside the compact set
union(j<i) x_j U U^(-1). Choose K_i witnessing that membership.
The second requirement follows. Local compactness and regularity
then give the required compact neighborhood V_i of x_i disjoint
from H. In particular x_i is in the interior of V_i, and later
K_j avoid V_i. The groups K_i are all distinct.

Let B={K_i:i>=1}. This is discrete in L(G): the neighborhood
D_2(interior(V_i)) of K_i meets B only among its first i members,
and the finitely many other members can be excluded because
L(G) is Hausdorff.

The set D={x_i:i>=1} is closed discrete in G. For details, choose
an open identity neighborhood Q with Q^(-1)Q contained in
U U^(-1). Every translate gQ contains at most one x_i, since
two such elements would have intersecting translates x_i U.
This provides local discreteness; at a point outside D one can
also remove the possible single x_i from the neighborhood. Thus
D is closed.

We claim B is closed in L(G). Suppose K is in its closure but
not in B. For each i the open set D_2(interior(V_i)) meets B
only finitely. It cannot contain K, since those finitely many
members could then be excluded to give a neighborhood of K
disjoint from B. Therefore K misses interior(V_i) for every i,
and in particular misses every x_i. Since D is closed, the
upper neighborhood D_1(G minus D) of K is open. It contains none
of the K_i, as K_i contains x_i. This is a contradiction.

Thus B is an infinite closed discrete subspace of L(G) contained
in compact F. It would itself be compact, which an infinite
discrete space is not. This final contradiction proves the lemma.

## 3. Every compact family has a clopen selector

Let F be a compact subspace of L(G) all of whose members are
noncompact subgroups. Set

    U_H = {K in F: K is contained in H}.

This set is closed by Section1. To prove it open, take any K in
U_H. The entire compact space F is a countably compact neighborhood
of K in F, and K is noncompact. Protasov's lemma supplies an open
neighborhood W of K in F contained in U_K. Since K is contained in
H, we have U_K contained in U_H. Thus every point of U_H has an
open neighborhood contained in U_H, proving openness.

The remaining selector properties are just subgroup inclusion:
H belongs to U_H; mutual containment gives equality; and
K in U_H implies U_K is contained in U_H. Therefore F has a
clopen selector. Any space homeomorphic to F has the pulled-back
selector.

This proves the necessary condition for any compact space in the
question. In particular the argument allows arbitrary weight and
nonabelian or nondiscrete ambient groups. It makes no separability,
metrizability, countability, or compact-generation assumption.

## 4. A height-four compact space without a selector

The construction is Dow--Watson's Example1, written out with an
explicit almost-disjoint family and a discrete witness set in the
contradiction argument. Reference: A. Dow and S. Watson, *Skula
spaces*, Commentationes Mathematicae Universitatis Carolinae
31 (1990), 27--31:
https://dml.cz/bitstream/handle/10338.dmlcz/106815/CommentatMathUnivCarol_031-1990-1_5.pdf .
The example and proof on page30 were read and visually checked.
The paper credits independent work of Bonnet, Rubin and Si-Kaddour
and an earlier construction of A. H. Stone. No novelty is claimed
for the space or its failure to have a selector.

### 4.1. The almost-disjoint family

Let omega={0,1,2,...}. For each function f:omega -> omega form the
infinite binary sequence

    b_f = 0^(f(0)) 1 0^(f(1)) 1 0^(f(2)) 1 ... .

Different functions give different binary sequences. Fix a bijection
between the finite binary words and omega. Let A_f be the set of
codes of all finite initial segments of b_f. Then A_f is an
infinite subset of omega and A_f intersect A_g is finite whenever
f and g are distinct: two different branches share only finitely
many initial segments.

### 4.2. The locally compact space before adding infinity

Start with Y=(omega+1) x omega, with the usual convergent-sequence
topology on omega+1 and the discrete topology on the second factor.
Write u_n=(omega,n) for the limit point of row n and v_(m,n)=(m,n)
for its isolated points. Each full row is compact and open in Y.

For every f:omega -> omega define the clopen subset of Y

    B_f = {u_n: n in A_f}
          union {v_(m,n): n in A_f and m>f(n)}.

Adjoin a point a_f for each f. The resulting space Z has Y as an
open subspace, retains the old neighborhoods at its points, and
has the following neighborhood base at a_f:

    C_(f,N) = {a_f} union
              {points of B_f in rows n>=N},       N in omega.

These sets are open by definition and by the openness of the row
tails in Y. They are compact: an open cover has a member containing
a_f and hence C_(f,M) for some M>=N; the uncovered remainder lies
in finitely many compact row tails.

The space Z is Hausdorff. Distinct points of Y can be separated in
Y. To separate a_f from a point of row n, omit that row in
C_(f,N). To separate a_f and a_g, omit all the finitely many rows
in A_f intersect A_g from both neighborhoods. This produces
disjoint neighborhoods. Consequently each compact C_(f,N) is
closed as well as open in Z.

Every point has a compact neighborhood: a full row for points
of Y, and C_(f,N) for a_f. Thus Z is locally compact Hausdorff.
The set A={a_f:f in omega^omega} is closed and discrete in Z,
because Y is open and C_(f,N) intersects A only in a_f. It is
infinite, so Z is not compact.

Let X=Z union {infinity} be the one-point compactification of Z.
It is compact Hausdorff. Neighborhoods of infinity are complements
of compact subsets of Z, together with infinity. Each such
neighborhood contains all but finitely many points of A: a compact
subset of Z meets the closed discrete set A in a compact discrete
set and hence in finitely many points.

### 4.3. The derived sets

The v_(m,n) are isolated in X. Each u_n is a limit of the isolated
points in its row, and every a_f is a limit of u_n with n in A_f.
Infinity is a limit of the infinite set A. It follows, using the
specified neighborhoods, that the successive derived sets are

    X'   = {u_n:n in omega} union A union {infinity},
    X''  = A union {infinity},
    X''' = {infinity},
    X''''= empty.

For example u_n is isolated in X' by its row neighborhood, and a_f
is isolated in X'' by C_(f,N). Every nonempty subset of X has a
point of least one of these four ranks, which is isolated within
that subset. Thus X is scattered, of height exactly four.

### 4.4. A selector would contradict its own antisymmetry

Suppose X has a clopen selector x -> U_x. Since U_(u_n) is a
neighborhood of u_n, there is an integer t(n) such that

    v_(m,n) belongs to U_(u_n) whenever m>t(n).

Choose any function f satisfying f(n)>t(n) for all n. Because
U_(a_f) is a neighborhood of a_f, for some N it contains C_(f,N).
Hence u_n belongs to U_(a_f) for every n in A_f with n>=N. The
selector's transitivity implies

    U_(u_n) is contained in U_(a_f) for all these n.

For each n in this infinite set choose m_n=t(n)+1. Then
t(n)<m_n<=f(n), so the point v_(m_n,n) lies in U_(u_n) and thus
in U_(a_f), while it does not belong to B_f. Put

    D_f = {v_(t(n)+1,n): n in A_f and n>=N}.

This is an infinite closed discrete subset of Z. Each row contains
at most one point of D_f, so there is no accumulation point in Y.
The neighborhood C_(f,0) of a_f is disjoint from D_f. If g differs
from f, the set D_f meets the rows of C_(g,0) in only finitely
many points, since A_f intersect A_g is finite; some C_(g,M)
therefore avoids D_f. This excludes accumulation at every a_g.

No compact subset of Z contains D_f, since a closed discrete
subspace of a compact space is finite. Therefore every neighborhood
of infinity in X meets D_f. As U_(a_f) is closed and contains D_f,
it follows that infinity belongs to U_(a_f).

There are infinitely many such functions f, for instance
f_k(n)=t(n)+k for positive integers k. The neighborhood U_infinity
contains all but finitely many of the points a_(f_k). Choose k
with a_(f_k) in U_infinity. We have also just proved that infinity
is in U_(a_(f_k)), contradicting selector condition2 for these two
distinct points. Thus X has no clopen selector.

## 5. Negative answer

If X embedded homeomorphically into the requested subgroup space
for any locally compact group G, its image F would be a compact
family of closed noncompact subgroups. Section3 would give F a
clopen selector and hence give X one. Section4 proves this
impossible. This settles the printed universal assertion negatively.

## 6. The precise positive class

Conversely, suppose X is compact Hausdorff and has a clopen selector
x -> U_x. Form the discrete abelian group

    G = Z direct sum (direct sum over y in X of C_2).

Write e_y for the generator of the y-th C_2 factor. For x in X set

    S_x = {y in X: x is not in U_y},
    H_x = Z direct sum (direct sum over y in S_x of C_2).

Every H_x is a closed noncompact subgroup of G, since G is discrete
and H_x contains its infinite cyclic direct summand. The discrete
group G is locally compact.

The map i:x -> H_x is injective. If x differs from z, condition2
gives, for example, x not in U_z or z not in U_x. In the first
case e_z lies in H_x but not H_z; the other case is symmetric.

For continuity of the lower Vietoris subbasis, fix g in G. Its
C_2 component has a finite support T. Then

    {x:g in H_x} = intersection over y in T of (X minus U_y),

a clopen set. Thus for any open subset O of the discrete G the
inverse image of D_2(O) is a union of open sets, one for each g in O.

For the upper subbasis, suppose H_x is contained in O. If z is in
U_x then H_z is contained in H_x. Indeed, x in U_y implies
U_x contained in U_y by selector condition3, and then z in U_y.
Taking contrapositives gives S_z contained in S_x. Consequently
U_x is a neighborhood of x on which H_z is contained in O.
This proves that the inverse image of D_1(O) is open as well.

The target subgroup space for discrete G is Hausdorff: distinct
subgroups are separated by containing or avoiding an element on
which they differ, both of which are Vietoris open conditions.
A continuous injection from compact X into this Hausdorff space
is a homeomorphism onto its image. This proves the positive
direction and the stated characterization.

The complement in the definition of S_x is essential. Assigning
H_x a new generator for each y in U_x would usually make the
image discrete and would fail to reproduce the topology at its
nonisolated points.
