# Problem 9.45: an explicit coordinate test for an orthogonal basis

**Status:** complete mathematical candidate with internal logical,
computational and source audits. Novelty and outside review are pending. This gives
necessary and sufficient coordinate conditions in every dimension.
Its running time depends on the numerical denominator, and is not
claimed polynomial in its binary encoding length.

## 1. The coordinate criterion

Given a in Q^n, subtract integer coordinates and write

    a = r/m,  0 <= r_j < m,  gcd(m,r_1,...,r_n)=1,

where m is the least positive common denominator. If m=1, S(a)=Z^n
has its standard orthogonal basis. The following test includes this
case without an exception in the formula.

For each k=1,...,m-1, put s_j(k)=k r_j mod m, in {0,...,m-1}.
Form the centered representatives w by the rules

    w_j = s_j(k)                  if 2 s_j(k) < m,
    w_j = s_j(k)-m                if 2 s_j(k) > m,
    w_j in {-m/2,m/2}             if 2 s_j(k) = m.

If there are more than two ties in the last rule, discard this k.
Otherwise there are at most four representatives. Keep only those
whose first nonzero coordinate is positive. For each remaining w,
set Q(w)=sum_j w_j^2 and apply the integer divisibility conditions

    Q(w) divides m w_j for every j,                 (D1)
    Q(w) divides sum_j r_j w_j.                     (D2)

Let W(r,m) be the list of representatives passing both tests, and
let z(r) be the number of zero coordinates of r.

**Theorem.** The necessary and sufficient condition requested in
Problem 9.45 is

    z(r) + |W(r,m)| = n.                           (C)

When (C) holds, an orthogonal Z-basis of S(a) consists of

    e_j for r_j=0,  together with w/m for w in W(r,m).

No search for a compatible subset of W is necessary: its vectors,
together with these coordinate vectors, are automatically mutually
orthogonal. There are never more than n altogether.

The instructions defining W are entirely in terms of the given
coordinates. They use m-1 residue vectors, at most four sign choices
per vector, and integer divisions. In particular (C) does not contain
an unknown basis, an unbounded Diophantine quantifier, or a call to a
lattice orthogonalization procedure.

## 2. Orthogonal rank-one summands

For a full-rank Euclidean lattice L, write

    L* = {c : x.c is an integer for every x in L}.

Call a nonzero b in L a splitting vector if c_b=b/||b||^2 belongs
to L*. Then

    L = Z b orthogonal-direct-sum (L intersect b-perp).     (1)

Indeed, every x in L has integer coefficient t=x.c_b, and
x-tb belongs to L and is perpendicular to b. The intersection of
the two summands is zero. Conversely, if (1) holds, projection onto
the first summand gives x.c_b in Z, so b is a splitting vector.
In particular every member of an orthogonal lattice basis splits.

**Lemma 1.** Two splitting vectors are either orthogonal or equal
up to sign.

Proof. For splitting vectors b and v the two numbers

    A = (b.v)/||b||^2,  B = (b.v)/||v||^2

are integers. If their lines are different, strict Cauchy--Schwarz
gives 0 <= AB < 1. Thus AB=0 and b.v=0. If the lines coincide,
write v=t b. The same integrality gives t and 1/t in Z; hence
t=1 or -1. This proves the lemma.

It follows that all splitting lines of L form a mutually orthogonal
family, of size at most n. If there are n, choose their generators
b_1,...,b_n. For any x in L, its orthogonal expansion is

    x = sum_i (x.c_{b_i}) b_i,

with all coefficients integral. Thus these generators form a
Z-basis. We have proved the general equivalence

    L has an orthogonal basis iff L has n splitting lines.   (2)

This also proves uniqueness of an orthogonal basis up to signs and
permutation, whenever it exists. The splitting argument is elementary
lattice theory; no novelty is claimed for that general observation.

## 3. The centered-representative bound

Take L=S(r/m). Since Z^n is contained in L, the dual L* is contained
in Z^n. If b splits, put c=b/||b||^2 in L* and d=||c||^2. Then

    c in Z^n,  d is a positive integer,  b=c/d.      (3)

If d=1, c is a signed coordinate vector. Therefore b=+/-e_j,
which splits exactly when r_j=0: the condition e_j in L* says
r_j/m is an integer.

Suppose d>=2. For every coordinate, 2|c_j|<=d. For |c_j|>=2
this follows from 2|c_j|<=c_j^2<=d; for |c_j|=1 it follows from
d>=2; and for zero coordinates it is immediate. Consequently

    |b_j| <= 1/2 for every j.                      (4)

Moreover there are at most two coordinates attaining equality.
If h of them do, then c_j^2=d^2/4 in these h positions, and

    h d^2/4 <= sum_j c_j^2=d,

so h d<=4 and h<=2. This bound justifies discarding cosets with
three or more half-coordinate ties. It also justifies examining all
remaining sign choices, rather than picking one arbitrary rounding.

The vector b is not an integer vector: its squared norm is 1/d<1,
whereas every nonzero integer vector has squared norm at least one.
Thus its class modulo Z^n is represented by exactly one k in
{1,...,m-1}. Existence follows from L=Z^n+Z(r/m); uniqueness follows
from the minimality of m. More explicitly, if m divides t r_j for
all j, a Bezout identity for gcd(m,r_1,...,r_n)=1 implies m divides t.

Combining this with (4), every noncoordinate splitting vector is
one of the centered representatives listed in Section 1, divided
by m. Its sign can be chosen to make its first nonzero coordinate
positive. This sign convention gives one representative per line.
Distinct k cannot produce the same w, and distinct tie choices for
one k cannot produce the same w; no hidden multiplicity remains.

## 4. Divisibility detects exactly the summands

For a listed w, set b=w/m. It belongs to L by construction. As
||b||^2=Q(w)/m^2, its prospective dual vector is

    c_b = m w/Q(w).

An arbitrary c belongs to L* if and only if

    c_j is an integer for all j, and (r/m).c is an integer. (5)

This is because the e_j and r/m generate L. Substitution of c_b
into (5) gives exactly (D1) and (D2). Thus every accepted vector
splits, and every splitting vector from Section 3 is accepted.

We have listed all splitting lines, without duplication. Applying
(2) proves the criterion (C), its basis formula, and the assertion
that all accepted vectors are automatically mutually orthogonal.
This completes the proof of the main theorem.

## 5. Size and implementation

Each residue vector requires O(n) elementary integer operations.
There are at most four choices per k. Divisibility testing each
choice takes O(n) operations. Counting the accepted representatives
therefore takes O(m n) arithmetic operations and O(n) working memory.
Returning dense basis vectors adds O(n^2) output work and storage.
The normalization from rational input uses gcd/lcm, not factorization.

Integers in the test have O(log(n)+log(m)) bits: |w_j|<=m/2,
Q<=n m^2/4, and |r.w|<n m^2/2. Thus the bit cost is also explicit
after choosing ordinary integer arithmetic. The numerical m may be
exponential in the input length. No polynomial-time claim in log m
or claim about general lattice isomorphism complexity is made.

The reference implementation is `scripts/orthogonal_9_45.py`.
It uses exact integers and rational numbers. Its first-positive
convention discards negative representatives instead of normalizing
them into duplicate positive representatives.

## 6. Examples and sign sensitivity

For a=(1/2,1/2), the unique nonzero coset has two half-coordinate
ties. It supplies the two accepted representatives (1,1) and
(1,-1), giving the basis (1/2,1/2),(1/2,-1/2). Keeping only one
rounding would incorrectly reject this lattice.

For a=(1/6,1/6), the accepted scaled vectors are (1,1) and (3,-3).
The half-coordinate choice (3,3) fails (D2), while (3,-3) passes.
For a=(1/6,5/6), the roles of these two tie choices reverse.

For a=(1/2,1/2,1/2), its nonzero coset has three half ties; there
are no splitting vectors and no orthogonal basis.

Composite denominators permit genuinely three-dimensional examples.
For a=(1,5,2)/6, the basis supplied is

    (1,-1,2)/6,  (1,-1,-1)/3,  (1,1,0)/2.

The corresponding dual basis consists of the integer rows

    (1,-1,2),  (1,-1,-1),  (1,1,0),

whose squared lengths are 6,3,2 and whose determinant has absolute
value 6. Their scalar products with r are 0,-6,6. This confirms
the required congruences and the covolume independently.
Its 3-primary intermediate lattice S(2a)=S((1,2,2)/3) is not
orthogonal, by the prime-power result below. Thus testing the
primary intermediate lattices separately is not a valid general rule.

The initially proposed residue (3,1,2)/6 was a transcription error:
it does not annihilate those integer rows, and both exact algorithms
reject it. Its failed expected-result log is retained. The algorithm
was not changed in response; the example was corrected.

## 7. A structural prime-power consequence

The following independent reduction explains why prime powers are
particularly restrictive. It is supplementary to the general test.

**Proposition.** Suppose m=p^k>1. If S(r/m) is orthogonal, at most
two entries of r are nonzero. A single nonzero entry always works.
With exactly two, denoted (r_i,r_j)=(s,t), orthogonality is equivalent
to the existence of an integer c in {1,...,k}, coprime integers u,v,
and a unit alpha modulo p^k such that

    u^2+v^2=p^c,  (s,t)=alpha(-v,u) modulo p^k.     (6)

Proof. The dual lattice is

    Lambda={z in Z^n : r.z=0 mod p^k}.

Its quotient in Z^n is cyclic of order p^k. Orthogonality passes
between any lattice and its dual: the dual of an orthogonal basis
b_i is b_i/||b_i||^2. Let C be an orthogonal integer row basis of
Lambda. Its determinant has absolute value p^k and

    C C^t = diag(d_1,...,d_n),  product_i d_i=p^(2k).

Each d_i is a power of p. The Smith normal form is diag(1,...,1,p^k),
so rank(C mod p)=n-1. The matrix rank inequality gives

    rank(C C^t mod p) >= n-2.

Therefore at most two d_i are divisible by p. Every other row has
squared length one, so is a signed coordinate vector. These unit
rows occupy distinct coordinates and force the corresponding
coordinates of r to vanish. This proves the support bound.

With one nonzero residue, it is a unit modulo p^k and the lattice
is the direct product of (1/p^k)Z on that axis and Z on all others.
With two residues, split off the other coordinate axes and work in
dimension two. Every orthogonal integer row pair has the form

    g(u,v),  h(-v,u),  gcd(u,v)=1,  g,h>0,

up to signs. Its determinant is g h (u^2+v^2)=p^k. Cyclicity of
the quotient implies gcd(g,h)=1, since the first Smith invariant
is the gcd of all four entries. Thus one of g,h equals one. Swap
and rotate the primitive pair if necessary to make g=1. We obtain
u^2+v^2=p^c and h=p^(k-c). The value c=0 gives only a coordinate
lattice, contrary to two nonzero residues. The primitive row (u,v)
belongs to Lambda precisely when (s,t) is a scalar multiple of
(-v,u) modulo p^k; the scalar is a unit because (s,t) is primitive
modulo p. This proves necessity of (6).

Conversely, the rows (u,v) and p^(k-c)(-v,u) have determinant p^k
and both satisfy the defining congruence under (6). They therefore
form an orthogonal basis of the index-p^k congruence lattice.
Dualizing proves sufficiency.

For p=2, a primitive sum of two squares equal to a positive power
of two must be 2: modulo 4 both entries must be odd, and their
squares sum to 2 modulo 4. Thus the two-residue case is exactly
two odd residues with t=+/-s modulo 2^k. For p=3 mod 4, there is
no primitive representation of p^c by two squares: otherwise -1
would be a square modulo p, or both entries would be divisible
by p. Only the one-residue case occurs. For other primes (6) is
already a finite explicit integer criterion; no Gaussian-factorization
theorem is needed for it.

More generally the same rank argument for every prime p dividing m
shows that an orthogonal S(r/m) has at most 2 omega(m) nonzero
coordinates, where omega counts distinct prime divisors. Each
nonunit squared row length has some prime divisor, and each prime
occurs in at most two row lengths. The remaining rows are unit
coordinate rows. This is necessary, not a claimed sufficient test.

## 8. Prior work and scope

V. G. Bardakov, *On orthogonal bases for rational lattices*, Siberian
Mathematical Journal 39 (1998), 1067--1079,
https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02674118, explicitly addresses Problem 9.45.
He gives the full n=2 criterion and a sufficient condition in general
dimension (pairwise coprime reduced coordinate denominators). The
introduction and Proposition 2 were visually checked. The present
criterion is not a claim to the first two-dimensional solution.

K. Chandrasekaran, V. Gandikota and E. Grigorescu, *Deciding
Orthogonality in Construction-A Lattices*, arXiv:1512.02734v1,
9 December 2015, later SIAM J. Discrete Math. 31 (2017), gives
structural characterizations and efficient algorithms for arbitrary
binary and ternary linear codes. Scaling S(r/p) by p yields their
Construction-A lattice for the one-dimensional code spanned by r.
Their results are prior, and their complexity setting differs from
an O(m n) test for a cyclic quotient of order m.

Their published Claims 3.1--3.2 also use the integrality of the
normalized dual basis to restrict coordinates. That general method
is prior and credited here. Both their original preprint and the
published article were consulted, with exact reading scopes in
`9.45-review.md`.

B. Hemkemeier and F. Vallentin, *Incremental Algorithms for Lattice
Problems*, ECCC TR98-052 revision 1 (11 September 2006), Section 4.3,
credits uniqueness of orthogonal decomposition to Eichler and gives
an algorithm implementing Kneser's argument from a complete bounded
set of lattice vectors. This is further prior context for the
splitting method. The cyclic-coset test above neither needs such a
complete generating set nor computes general indecomposable factors.
https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/1998/052/

None of these papers is imported as a proof input for the main criterion.
The theorem above is proved in full. Further novelty checking is
required, particularly for algorithms using orthogonal summands or
centered representatives of finite quotient lattices. The mathematical
criterion is complete; its historical priority is not established.
