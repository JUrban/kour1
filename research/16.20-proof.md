# Problem 16.20: a finite central construction

Status: complete candidate affirmative answer, with exact computational
controls and a separate internal audit in `16.20-review.md`. Novelty
remains unconfirmed, and no outside review has taken place.

## Target and interpretation

Problem 16.20 asks whether the dominions of a fixed H<=A, as the
quasivariety N varies below a fixed quasivariety M, can form a modular
but nondistributive lattice under inclusion. The definition is by pairs
of homomorphisms A -> B with B in N that agree on H.

The original lattice literature explicitly allows A not in N: see
Shakhova (2005), p.239, and Budkin (2008), pp.542--543, cited in the
audit. The construction below also works if only subquasivarieties
containing A are admitted. Under
the latter convention it gives exactly the five-element diamond M_3.
Under the former it gives the interval of all normal subgroups of A
containing H, which is modular and contains that diamond as an interval.

Everything uses finite groups, and M itself can be generated as a
quasivariety by a single finite group.

## A transfer observation

For a class C of groups let q(C) be the quasivariety it generates.
If A is finite, H<=A, and a in A, the condition

    every pair of homomorphisms A -> B agreeing on H agrees at a

is a quasiidentity in the variable group B. Indeed, introduce two
variables X_g,Y_g for each g in A. In the premise put all multiplication
table relations X_g X_h=X_(gh), Y_g Y_h=Y_(gh), and X_h=Y_h for
h in H. In the conclusion put X_a=Y_a. This is a finite universal
Horn sentence and encodes exactly the stated condition. Identity
images follow already from the multiplication relations.

Consequently, dominion in q(C) is determined by the groups in C:

    dom_(q(C))^A(H) = intersection_(B in C) dom_B^A(H),

where on the right only maps into that particular target B are tested.
This also applies to finite C. It avoids any finiteness assumption on
the groups that subsequently occur in q(C).

## Seven perfect factors

For i=1,...,7 take U_i=SL_2(F_(p_i)), where

    (p_1,...,p_7)=(7,11,13,17,19,23,29).

Write z_i=-I for the central involution of U_i and S_i=PSL_2(F_(p_i)).
The standard facts required are that U_i is perfect, its centre is
<z_i> of order two, and S_i is nonabelian simple. Their orders are

    |S_i| = 168, 660, 1092, 2448, 3420, 6072, 12180.

For distinct i,j, |S_i| does not divide |S_j|. This is an explicit
integer check on the displayed list. Thus every homomorphism
U_i -> S_j is trivial for i!=j. To justify this, a proper normal
subgroup R of U_i must be central: otherwise R Z(U_i)=U_i, and
perfectness gives U_i=[U_i,U_i]<=R. Hence a nontrivial image of U_i
has order |U_i| or |S_i|. Neither divides |S_j|.

## The central quotient

Let W=F_2^3. Enumerate its seven nonzero vectors as

    v_1=e_1, v_2=e_2, v_3=e_3, v_4=e_1+e_2,
    v_5=e_1+e_3, v_6=e_2+e_3, v_7=e_1+e_2+e_3.

In the centre Z=product_i <z_i> of U=product_i U_i, set

    C = { product_i z_i^(epsilon_i) : sum_i epsilon_i v_i = 0 }.

The map Z -> W is onto, so |C|=2^4. Define A=U/C. The images of
the individual factors U_i embed, since C intersects each <z_i>
trivially. We use U_i for these embedded images as well. They commute
with one another, and A is their central product. Its centre is
exactly W=Z/C, with z_i mapping to v_i, because

    A/W = product_i S_i

has trivial centre. The group A is perfect and has order

    |A| = product_i p_i(p_i^2-1) / 16
        = 599766788342131458048000.

Fix H=<e_1><=W<=A, of order two.

## Normal subgroups and maps to quotients

Every normal subgroup N of A has the form

    N = A_I K,  where A_I=product_(i in I) U_i,
    K=N intersect W, and span{v_i : i in I} <= K <= W.

Here is a proof. The image of N in product S_i is a product of a
subset I of those simple factors. For each i in I, [N,U_i] is
contained in N intersect U_i and maps onto [S_i,S_i]=S_i. A normal
subgroup of the perfect central cover U_i mapping onto S_i must be
all of U_i, by the same argument as above. Hence A_I<=N. Removing
those components from an element of N leaves an element of N intersect
W, proving the asserted description.

We claim that for every normal N of A, every homomorphism

    f:A -> A/N

kills N. Put K=N intersect W and use its associated set I. The
quotient B=A/N has central subgroup W/K and quotient product_(j notin I)
S_j. For each i, restrict f to U_i and project to these S_j. By the
order nondivisibility, every projection with j!=i is trivial. Thus
if i in I, the image f(U_i) is central and is trivial by perfectness.

For i notin I, let D_i denote the image of the original U_i in B.
The image f(U_i) lies in D_i Z(B), and it is perfect, so

    f(U_i) <= [D_i Z(B),D_i Z(B)] = D_i.

The equality holds because D_i is a quotient of a perfect group.
If v_i in K, then D_i is S_i. Every homomorphism U_i -> S_i kills
z_i: it is either trivial or onto with kernel <z_i>. Therefore f
kills v_i for every i with v_i in K, including the already killed
factors indexed by I. All nonzero vectors of W occur among the v_i,
so these v_i generate K. We conclude that f kills both A_I and K,
and hence kills N.

The assertions about the centre used here are exact: the centre of
B is W/K, because its quotient product_(j notin I) S_j is centreless.
The preimage of an individual S_i is D_i Z(B), as used above.

## Endomorphisms on the centre

An endomorphism f of A maps each U_i into itself, by the preceding
projection and perfectness argument with N=1. It is either trivial
on U_i or induces a surjection onto S_i. In the latter case, if its
image is J, then U_i=J Z(U_i); perfectness implies U_i=J, so this
restriction is an automorphism. Thus f(v_i) is either 0 or v_i.

Since every nonzero vector of W is one of the v_i, the induced linear
map on W is either zero or the identity. Indeed, if it fixed a nonzero
u and killed a nonzero v, these vectors would be distinct, and its
value u at u+v would be neither zero nor u+v, a contradiction.

It follows that

    dom_(q(A))^A(H) = W.

For the inclusion W in the dominion, take two endomorphisms of A
agreeing on the nonzero subgroup H. Their restrictions to W, each
zero or identity, must be equal. The transfer observation then gives
the same conclusion for every target in q(A). For the reverse
inclusion, every inner automorphism of A fixes the central subgroup
H. Comparing it with the identity shows that any element of the
dominion must commute with all of A, and hence belongs to W.

## A finite ambient quasivariety

Let

    M = q({A/N : N normal in A}).

There are only finitely many normal subgroups. Equivalently M is
generated by the finite direct product of all these quotients: each
quotient embeds as a factor of that product, and quasivarieties are
closed under products. In particular A belongs to M, and M satisfies
the finite-exponent identity of A, so it is a proper quasivariety of
groups.

## If only quasivarieties containing A are allowed

Any N<=M containing A has

    H <= dom_N^A(H) <= W,

where the second inclusion follows by comparing the identity and
inner automorphisms into the target A. Its dominion is a subgroup,
being an intersection of equalizer subgroups, hence it is a vector
subspace of W containing H.

Conversely, let H<=K<=W and set N_K=q(A,A/K), which contains A and
is contained in M. Every map A -> A/K kills K by the quotient-map
claim proved above. Comparing the canonical quotient with the trivial
map gives the converse bound, since these two maps agree on H. Thus

    dom_(A/K)^A(H)=K.

The transfer observation and dom_(q(A))^A(H)=W give

    dom_(N_K)^A(H)=W intersect K=K.

Therefore the entire family of admissible dominions is exactly the
interval [H,W] of vector subspaces. This is the lattice of subspaces
of W/H=F_2^2, namely M_3: a bottom, three incomparable middle elements,
and a top. It is modular and nondistributive.

Explicitly, the three intermediate subspaces can be taken as

    K_1=<e_1,e_2>, K_2=<e_1,e_3>, K_3=<e_1,e_2+e_3>.

They pairwise intersect in H and pairwise sum to W, displaying failure
of the distributive law.

## If all subquasivarieties are allowed

For every class N of target groups, dom_N^A(H) is a normal subgroup
of A containing H. It is a subgroup as above. For normality, if a
belongs to the dominion, g in A, and f_1,f_2:A -> B agree on H, then
f_1 composed with conjugation by g and f_2 composed with that same
conjugation also agree on H, because H is central. Thus they agree
at a, which says the original maps agree at a^g.

Conversely, for every normal N of A containing H, all maps A -> A/N
kill N by the quotient-map claim. The canonical quotient and the
trivial map agree on H and have equalizer N. The transfer observation
therefore gives

    dom_(q(A/N))^A(H)=N.

As q(A/N)<=M, all such normal subgroups occur. The entire dominion
family is consequently the normal subgroup interval [H,A]. The
normal subgroup lattice of a group is modular (the Dedekind modular
identity, with joins given by products of normal subgroups). The
interval [H,W] inside it is the nondistributive diamond just displayed.
Thus this convention also gives a modular nondistributive lattice.

## A smaller example for the original convention

For the original convention allowing A not in N, the same construction
needs only three factors and permits H=1. Namely, set

    U_1=SL_2(5), U_2=SL_2(7), U_3=SL_2(13),
    A_0=(U_1 x U_2 x U_3)/<z_1 z_2 z_3>, H_0=1.

The orders of the simple quotients are 60,168,1092; none divides
another. The centre W_0 has order four, with images of z_1,z_2,z_3
its three distinct nonidentity elements. The order of A_0 is

    120 * 336 * 2184 / 2 = 44,029,440.

The normal-subgroup classification and quotient-map argument above
apply verbatim with these three factors and W_0=F_2^2. In detail,
every normal R has the form A_I K, with K=R intersect W_0 containing
the central images of the factors indexed by I. For f:A_0 -> A_0/R,
order nondivisibility makes every cross-factor projection trivial.
Perfectness kills the removed factors and places every surviving
factor image inside its corresponding perfect factor. Whenever the
central element v_i lies in K, that surviving factor is S_i, so f
kills v_i. Since every nonzero vector of W_0 is one of these three
v_i, f kills K and therefore R.

Take M_0=q({A_0/R : R normal in A_0}). For any target class the
dominion of 1 is the intersection of kernels of all homomorphisms
into that class: comparison with the trivial map proves this directly.
It is therefore normal. For each R, all maps A_0 -> A_0/R kill R,
while the canonical map has exactly that kernel. The finite Horn
transfer gives

    dom_(q(A_0/R))^(A_0)(1)=R.

Thus the entire dominion family is the normal subgroup lattice of
A_0. This lattice is modular, and its central interval [1,W_0] is
M_3. This is a second, smaller affirmative example for the printed
question. It does not claim the five-element lattice under the
additional requirement A_0 in N; the seven-factor example supplies
that stronger statement.

There are 15 normal subgroups: five central subgroups; for each of
three individual factors, two choices of K (its central line or W_0);
three subgroups containing exactly two factors, each with K=W_0;
and the whole group. GAP constructed A_0 itself and independently
found exactly these 15 normal subgroups. Its direct subgroup operations
pass 1,305 modular-law checks and exhibit the central diamond.

## Verification and scope

`scripts/verify_16_20.g` checks all seven perfect central covers, their
normal subgroups and simple quotients, and the required order
nondivisibilities. `scripts/verify_16_20.py` checks the central code,
all 199 normal-subgroup labels, 2,609 possible factor supports over all
quotients, and the 154-element interval above H, including 584,430
modular identities. It also checks the five central dominions in the
restricted convention. `scripts/verify_16_20_small.g` constructs the
smaller group and checks its actual normal-subgroup lattice. All saved
logs end in PASS.

The classification and universal homomorphism argument are proved
structurally; no exhaustive enumeration of all homomorphisms of these
groups is claimed. The internal audit records the printed-statement
check, source-reading scope, and remaining novelty uncertainty.
