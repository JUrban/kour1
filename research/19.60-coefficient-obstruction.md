# 19.60: a prior obstruction for arbitrary computable coefficient PIDs

11 September 2026. This is a precise qualification to the printed
question, based on a classical computable-field theorem. It is
excluded from the possible-new candidate count. It does not decide
the usual matrix-group freeness problem over Z or Q.

## Statement with an effective coefficient model

The printed question on page 139 allows a principal ideal domain R,
any positive dimension d, and a finite tuple X_1,...,X_m in SL(d,R)
or GL(d,R), and asks for a decision algorithm for free generation.
No restriction to finitely generated or arithmetic coefficient
domains is printed.

With the usual meaning of a computable ring (decidable equality and
computable arithmetic on finite codes), the assertion for arbitrary
such R is false:

**Proposition. There is a fixed countable computable Euclidean domain
R of characteristic zero, with computable polynomial division, for
which no algorithm decides whether a single given matrix in SL(2,R)
freely generates an infinite cyclic group. The same holds for
GL(2,R), and for GL(1,R).**

The counterexample R can be chosen to be a polynomial ring F[t]
over a computable subfield F of C. Thus it is not necessary to rely
on the convention that fields themselves are PIDs.

## The imported theorem

Stoltenberg-Hansen and Tucker constructed a computable field F for
which the set U(F) of roots of unity is undecidable. More generally
it can have any recursively enumerable Turing degree. See
*Computing roots of unity in fields*, Bull. London Math. Soc.
12(6) (1980), 463–471, [publication record and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms/12.6.463).
The original 1979 author preprint is available from
[CWI, report IW 106/79](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/9392/9392D.pdf),
Theorem 3.4 on printed page 10, with its construction in Section 3.

The effective presentation here includes a decision procedure for
equality, not only enumeration of field equations. Inversion on
nonzero elements is computable: enumerate field elements b until
ab=1. Therefore the usual field operations needed for matrix
arithmetic are all available.

## Reduction to one matrix

Fix such a computable F and let R=F[t]. Represent polynomials by
finite coefficient lists. Equality, addition and multiplication
are computable; ordinary division with remainder is computable
using inversion of the nonzero leading coefficient. Degree is a
Euclidean function, so R is a PID with an effective Euclidean
algorithm.

For a nonzero element a of F, regard a and a^-1 as constant
polynomials and effectively form

    X(a) = diag(a,a^-1) in SL(2,R).

For every positive integer n,

    X(a)^n = diag(a^n,a^-n),
    X(a)^n=I  if and only if  a^n=1.

Thus the one-element tuple (X(a)) freely generates a rank-one free
group exactly when a is not a root of unity. A decision algorithm
for these tuples would decide U(F): handle a=0 separately, and for
a!=0 construct X(a) and negate the freeness answer. This contradicts
the choice of F.

If desired, also handle a=1 before constructing the input. All
remaining matrices are nonidentity, so no convention about a trivial
free group affects the reduction. A nonidentity finite cyclic group
is never a free group, while an infinite cyclic group is free of
rank one.

The identical matrices are valid inputs to GL(2,R). For GL(1,R),
use the one-by-one matrix (a): each nonzero constant is a unit of
F[t], and the same equivalence holds. Adding identity blocks also
extends the SL(2,R) obstruction to every fixed d>=2.

## Why this is a coefficient qualification

The field F and its effective presentation are fixed once and for
all; they are not varying parts of the input. No oracle for the
halting problem is provided. Equality and every ordinary arithmetic
operation are decidable, as is Euclidean division in R. The failure
is that these operations do not uniformly decide which elements
have finite multiplicative order.

For a fixed individual a, the small field Q(a) has a separate
effective description once its algebraic or transcendental nature
is supplied. Such descriptions are not uniformly recoverable from
arbitrary F-codes. This is why the obstruction is compatible with
familiar positive order algorithms over standard coefficient fields.

The proposition refutes an algorithm covering all computable PIDs
in the literal printed generality. It supplies no negative result
for R=Z, R=Q, number fields, or a model in which a transcendence or
roots-of-unity oracle is additionally supplied. The distinct and
substantive free-generation questions over those familiar rings
remain unresolved by this argument.

## Source audit

All twelve numbered pages of the 1979 CWI preprint were read,
including the computational definition, algebraic lemmas, the
staged construction, and the concluding remarks. The characteristic
zero construction and the lower reduction in Lemma 3.3 are the
parts needed here. Their ideal-membership argument preserves every
earlier finite-degree test by choosing a sufficiently high-degree
cyclotomic relation in a previously unrestricted variable. The
quotient is a computable domain and passing to its fraction field
preserves the lower reduction. Its earlier standard computable-field
and polynomial-ideal results remain imported.

There is a concrete error in the preprint's auxiliary Lemma 1.4:
printed page 5 uses n-1<=deg(p) for an arbitrary n-th root of unity.
The correct necessary inequality is phi(n)<=deg(p). The faulty
line was visually checked in the scanned page. It is not needed
for the undecidability direction used above. Even for the paper's
other direction it has an elementary repair: phi(n)^2>=n/2 gives
n<=2 deg(p)^2. To verify that estimate, write

    phi(n)^2/n = product over p^e || n of p^(e-2)(p-1)^2.

Every odd-prime factor is at least one; the factor for p=2 is
at least 1/2. The proposed corrected search bound therefore holds.
The final 1980 typeset article was not obtained, so no assertion is
made about whether the line was corrected there.

The 1999 handbook chapter by the same authors, *Computable rings
and fields*, DOI 10.1016/S0049-237X(99)80028-7, was located. An
indexed publisher excerpt gives Theorem 3.4.1 and a corrected
degree-dependent finite bound. The whole handbook chapter was not
obtained and is not used as a substitute for the CWI construction.

No numerical experiment could establish this undecidability
claim. The proof consists of the classical field construction and
the explicit effective diagonal reduction.
