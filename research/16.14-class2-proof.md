# 16.14: the bound in class two, at arbitrary exponent

September 12, 2026. Elementary alternative for class two, with a stronger
bound. The original inequality in all classes already follows from the
prior theorem recorded in `16.14-prior-result.md`. No new complete
solution or priority is claimed; outside review remains pending.

## The theorem

Let G be a finite 2-group of nilpotency class at most two, and suppose
every involution of G is central. Write Z=Z(G), Phi=Phi(G), and put

    r = rank(Z) = dim_F2(Z/Z^2),
    c = dim_F2 Z/(Z intersection Phi).

Then

    d(G) + c <= 2r.                                      (1)

In particular d(G)<=2 rank(Z), as requested in Problem 16.14. There
is no assumption on the exponent of G or the exponent of its derived
subgroup. This includes the previous exponent-four case: if a group
has exponent dividing four and all its involutions are central, its
squares are central and its central quotient has exponent two, so
it has class at most two.

All dimensions below are dimensions of finite elementary abelian
2-groups. Products in them may equivalently be written as sums.

## The square map on the central quotient

The group A=G/Z is abelian by the class assumption. Let V=A[2] be its
subgroup of elements of order dividing two. Write n=dim V; since A
is a finite abelian 2-group, also n=dim(A/A^2). This equality follows
on each cyclic direct factor and hence on their product.

The Frattini subgroup of a finite 2-group is Phi=G^2[G,G]=G^2.
Here G^2 denotes the subgroup generated by all squares. The final
equality follows because the quotient by G^2 has exponent two and
therefore is abelian. Put

    S = (Z intersection Phi)/Z^2.

It is a subgroup of the elementary abelian group Z/Z^2, of dimension
s=r-c. Indeed Z^2 is contained in Z intersection Phi, and the quotient
of Z/Z^2 by S is Z/(Z intersection Phi).

For xZ in V define

    q(xZ) = x^2 Z^2 in S.                               (2)

The condition xZ in V means precisely that x^2 belongs to Z. It
also belongs to Phi, so the value lies in S. Replacing x by xz,
z in Z, multiplies x^2 by z^2; hence (2) is well-defined.

It has exactly one zero. If q(xZ)=0, then x^2 is in Z^2. In an
abelian group the squares form a subgroup consisting of individual
squares, so there exists z in Z with z^2=x^2. Since z is central,
(xz^-1)^2=1. The hypothesis implies xz^-1 is central, including
the case where it is the identity. Thus x belongs to Z and xZ=0.
Conversely q(0)=0.

The same argument shows a useful equivalence for every class-two
finite 2-group, without assuming the hypothesis: q has only the
zero vector as a zero if and only if every involution is central.
For the reverse direction, a noncentral involution x gives the
nonzero vector xZ with q(xZ)=0.

## Quadraticity and the dimension bound

Use the commutator convention [x,y]=x^-1 y^-1 xy. In class two,

    (xy)^2 = x^2 y^2 [y,x].

Consequently the polarization of q is

    b(xZ,yZ) = [y,x] Z^2 in S.

Commutators are central, and the class-two commutator identities
make this map additive in both entries. It is independent of the
chosen lifts. Since V has exponent two it is bilinear over F_2.
In particular, for a basis v_1,...,v_n of V and binary coordinates
t_i, the values of q satisfy

    q(sum_i t_i v_i)
      = sum_i t_i q(v_i) + sum_(i<j) t_i t_j b(v_i,v_j).

In any basis of S, each coordinate is a polynomial of degree at
most two. This description allows linear terms and does not
assume the commutators or squares in G themselves have order two.

We prove n<=2s by an elementary zero count. If n>2s, the parity
of the number of zeros of the s coordinate polynomials q_j is

    sum_(t in F_2^n) product_(j=1)^s (1+q_j(t)).

The product has degree at most 2s<n. Each of its monomials omits
some variable, so its values sum to zero over F_2^n by pairing the
two choices of that variable. The total number of zeros is thus
even, contradicting the unique zero proved above. This argument
also covers s=0 when n>0. If n=0 the inequality is immediate.

## Return to the generator number

The image of Phi in A is A^2. The quotient map therefore gives the
exact sequence of elementary abelian groups

    0 -> Z/(Z intersection Phi) -> G/Phi -> A/A^2 -> 0.

To check the kernel explicitly, the inverse image of A^2 is ZPhi:
if xZ=(yZ)^2, then x lies in y^2 Z, which is contained in Phi Z.
Thus the kernel is ZPhi/Phi, as displayed. Burnside's basis theorem
now gives

    d(G) = c+n <= c+2s = 2r-c.

This is (1). All the use of arbitrary central direct factors is
accounted for by c; replacing d(G) by the rank of G/Z would lose
this term.

There is also a sectional-rank consequence. Every subgroup H of G
still has class at most two and all its involutions are central in
G, hence in H. The involutions of Z(H) form a subgroup of the
involutions of Z(G), so rank(Z(H))<=r. Applying the theorem to H
gives d(H)<=2r. Quotients of H need no more generators than H.
Thus every section of G has generator number at most 2 rank(Z(G)).

## Sharpness and limitations

For integers u,v>=0 take

    G = Q_8^u x C_(2^e_1) x ... x C_(2^e_v),   e_i>=1.

Every involution is central, the group has class at most two,
d(G)=2u+v, r=u+v, and c=v. Thus equality holds in (1), with
arbitrarily large exponent. Taking v=0 shows that the coefficient
two in the original bound cannot be reduced even in class two.

The assumption that A=G/Z is abelian is used both to make V a
vector space and to identify dim V with dim A/A^2. In a higher-class
group the set of involutions of G/Z need not be a subgroup. No
replacement argument is provided by this elementary proof. The separate
prior centralizer bound covers all classes for the original inequality.
The stronger inequality (1) is proved here only in class two; finite
controls do not imply any larger-class version of (1).

The proof is elementary apart from the standard finite-abelian
decomposition and Frattini/basis facts used explicitly above. The
parity lemma is a special case of the usual polynomial zero-count
argument, written out here. No claim that the class-two consequence
is new in the literature is made.
